The problem is not making a bad decision.
It is not knowing why we made it.

Organisations measure the outcomes of decisions. Far less often do they preserve the reasoning that existed when those decisions were made.

Organisations are built to track outcomes. Dashboards, KPIs, performance metrics, revenue. But where is the decision preserved? The principle of Decision Traceability introduces something different: the capacity to learn not only from what happened, but from how decisions were actually made.

The organisation knows the outcome.

The project worked.

The transformation failed.

The new executive delivered.

The acquisition did not produce the expected value.

The market chosen proved to be the right one.

The strategy was abandoned.

Months or years after an important decision, the outcome is often visible.

Far less visible is what existed in the moment that decision was made.

What information did we have?

What did we not have?

What assumptions were we making?

What alternatives had we considered?

What risks had we identified?

Which had been ignored?

What would have changed our mind?

What part of the decision came from data?

What from experience?

What from judgment?

What from organisational pressure?

Over time, this information tends to disappear.

The outcome remains.

And when only the outcome remains, one of the most important problems in organisational learning emerges:

we judge the decision already knowing how it ended.

The outcome rewrites the past

When we know how a decision turned out, it becomes extremely difficult to reconstruct what we knew before.

After a failure, certain signals suddenly seem obvious.

"It was clear it wasn't going to work."

"We should have seen it coming."

"The numbers were right there."

"The candidate wasn't right for the role."

"The market was already shifting."

But many of these conclusions carry an advantage the original decision never had:

they know the future.

At the moment the decision was made, that future did not yet exist.

What existed were incomplete information. Probabilities. Hypotheses. Contradictory signals. Alternatives. Uncertainty.

A decision should therefore be evaluated using what was reasonably knowable at the time it was made.

Not using information that became available afterwards.

"A past decision should not be judged with information that belongs to the future."

Outcome and Decision Quality are not the same thing

There is a fundamental distinction to be made.

The quality of an outcome and the quality of the decision that preceded it are not necessarily the same.

An excellent decision can produce a negative outcome.

A weak decision can produce a positive one.

Because between the decision and the outcome lies something no organisation controls completely:

the world.

Competitors. Markets. People. Timing. Technology. Geopolitics. Regulation. Unforeseen events. Luck.

A good decision increases the probability of a desirable outcome.

It cannot guarantee it.

Equally, a positive outcome does not automatically demonstrate that the decision-making process was sound.

The most dangerous quadrant is not always failure

Failure is visible.

It forces the organisation to ask questions.

Luck is harder to recognise.

A weak decision that produces a good outcome can be extremely dangerous because it validates the process that generated it.

The organisation concludes: "We decided well."

The leader concludes: "My judgment was sound."

The team concludes: "This approach works."

But perhaps the positive outcome arrived not because of the quality of the decision, but in spite of it.

The consequences are significant.

The process gets replicated.

Confidence increases.

Assumptions are reinforced.

The next decision is made the same way.

Until the moment luck stops compensating for the weakness of the process.

This is why a success can teach the wrong lesson.

And a failure can teach the right one.

Organisations have memory of outcomes

Dashboards. KPIs. Budgets. Revenue. EBITDA. Market share. Performance. Retention. Time-to-hire. Turnover. ROI.

Organisations are extraordinarily equipped to preserve outcomes.

But where is the decision preserved?

Where can we see, six months later:

what problem we were trying to solve;

which assumptions we considered true;

which information carried the most weight;

which alternatives were discarded;

why they were discarded;

what level of risk we considered acceptable;

which scenario we thought most likely;

what would have invalidated the decision?

Very often this structure does not exist.

There are presentations. Emails. Meeting notes. Messages. Spreadsheets. Memories.

But not necessarily a structured representation of the decision logic.

The outcome gets recorded.

The decision evaporates.

"Organisations build a memory of outcomes.
Decision Intelligence requires a memory of decisions as well."

Decision Traceability

Decision Traceability does not mean recording every conversation.

It does not mean creating more bureaucracy.

It does not mean slowing down management.

It means preserving the essential elements of a decision while they are still visible.

Before the outcome modifies them in our memory.

A strategic decision should be able to leave at least a trace of:

Why recording what we do not know matters

Organisations tend to document what they know.

But to properly evaluate a decision in the future, it is equally important to record what they did not know.

At the time of the decision:

What information was missing?

What variables were genuinely unforeseeable?

Which assumptions carried low confidence?

What scenarios were plausible?

This makes it possible to distinguish later:

errors of judgment from inevitable uncertainty.

The distinction is fundamental.

If an organisation interprets every negative outcome as an error, it risks becoming excessively cautious.

If it interprets every positive outcome as proof of competence, it risks becoming excessively confident.

Both reduce the quality of future decisions.

The problem of reconstructed decisions

After months, people rarely remember a decision exactly as it was made.

Memory reconstructs.

The outcome enters the narrative.

Initial beliefs become simplified.

Uncertainties are forgotten.

Doubts seem smaller in retrospect.

Evidence that proved correct gains importance.

Evidence that proved incorrect gets minimised.

The result is a tidy version of the past.

But real decisions are rarely tidy.

They are made under pressure. With incomplete information. Between competing interests. Inside shifting contexts.

Preserving the decision before the outcome allows us to compare two realities:

WHAT WE EXPECTED vs WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

And it is precisely in the distance between these two realities that learning can emerge.

Without a trace, there is no real decision learning

Suppose an organisation appoints a new CEO.

Eighteen months later, performance falls short of expectations.

The organisation asks: "Why did the CEO fail?"

But this question arrives too late in the causal chain.

A deeper decision review should be able to return to the original moment.

What problem was that appointment meant to solve?

What context had been defined?

What capabilities were considered essential?

What assumptions had been made about the market?

What risks had been identified in the candidate?

Which had been accepted?

What scenarios had been tested?

What changed subsequently?

Only then can we distinguish:

the wrong candidate; a poorly defined role; a changed context; an erroneous assumption; a known risk that materialised; an unforeseeable event.

Without this distinction, "bad hire" becomes an explanation.

But not necessarily a diagnosis.

Executive Hiring: from track record to decision record

In executive recruiting, a great deal is preserved about the candidate.

CV. Assessment. Interview notes. References. Compensation. Background.

But how much is preserved about the organisation's decision?

Why did that position exist?

What outcomes was it meant to produce?

What trade-offs had been accepted?

Why was that particular candidate chosen?

What risk was deliberately accepted?

What organisational conditions were necessary for success?

This information constitutes something different from the candidate record.

It constitutes the DECISION RECORD.

The candidate record describes the person.

The decision record describes why the organisation chose that person for that role, in that context, at that moment.

This distinction is central to the RATIO principle: ROLE FIRST.

From blame to learning

When a decision produces a negative outcome, the organisational question often defaults to:

"Who made the mistake?"

But this question can drastically reduce the value of the analysis.

A Decision Review should ask:

"What did we have good reason to believe when we decided?"

"Which assumption proved wrong?"

"Which signal did we misread?"

"What was genuinely unforeseeable?"

"Which part of the process should we change?"

This shifts the organisation:

FROM BLAME → TO LEARNING

Accountability does not disappear.

It becomes more precise.

Five questions for a Decision Review

1 of 5
What did we know at the time of the decision?
Do not use information that became available afterwards.
2 of 5
Which assumptions were decisive?
Identify those that, if false, would have changed the decision.
3 of 5
What did we expect to happen?
Without an initial expectation, it is difficult to measure the distance from the actual outcome.
4 of 5
What changed?
Separate decision error from contextual change.
5 of 5
What would we change in the process, not just in the decision?
Real learning is not saying: "next time we will choose differently." It is understanding: "next time we will decide better."

AI and decision memory

AI will progressively make it easier to store, organise and compare large amounts of information.

But storing information is not automatically the same as creating decision memory.

A system can remember everything that was said.

That does not mean it knows which assumptions actually determined a choice.

It can archive a hundred documents.

That does not mean it knows which evidence carried the most weight.

It can reconstruct a meeting.

That does not mean it knows why the Board decided.

Technology can make the trace more accessible.

But the decision structure must be defined first.

Otherwise we risk building perfect archives of decision-making processes we continue not to understand.

From Decision Outcome to Decision Quality

Mature organisations should not only ask:

"Did it work?"

They should be able to ask:

"Was it a good decision given the information available at the time?"

These are two different questions.

The first measures the outcome.

The second measures the quality of judgment.

And it is only by comparing them that we can genuinely learn.

Outcome tells us
What Happened

A decision should outlive its outcome

Every important decision should leave behind enough structure to be examined when its outcome becomes known.

Not to build an archive.

Not to assign blame.

But to prevent the outcome from completely rewriting the process that preceded it.

Because without this trace, organisations risk learning:

from positive outcomes that they were always right;

from negative outcomes that someone necessarily made a mistake.

Both can be false conclusions.

"The outcome tells us what happened.
The decision trace allows us to understand what we can learn."

Before the outcome, the decision

Decision Intelligence does not only serve to make better decisions.

It serves to make it possible to learn from them.

This requires something that normally disappears over time:

the decision before it knew its own outcome.

What information we had.

What assumptions we were making.

What alternatives existed.

What risk we had accepted.

What we expected.

Why we chose.

When this structure remains visible, the organisation can finally compare:

what it thought would happen with what actually happened.

And transform every important decision into something more than an outcome.

Into learning.
