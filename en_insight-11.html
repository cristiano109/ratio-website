Why More Data Doesn't Necessarily Lead to Better Executive Decisions

The challenge facing modern organisations is no longer access to information. It is understanding which information deserves to influence a decision.

Between the data and the decision lies something no dashboard can eliminate: judgment.

The challenge facing modern organisations is no longer access to information. It is understanding which information deserves to influence a decision.

For decades, one of the primary constraints on executive decision-making was the scarcity of information.

Leaders worked with incomplete data.

Analysis took time.

Information was distributed across different people, functions and systems.

Many decisions had to be made relying on experience, intuition and an inevitably limited body of evidence.

Today, the problem is changing.

Real-time dashboards.

Business Intelligence.

Predictive analytics.

Benchmarks.

Customer data.

People analytics.

Financial models.

Market intelligence.

And, increasingly, Artificial Intelligence systems capable of analysing vast quantities of information in seconds.

An executive today may have access to more data than an entire organisation could have processed just a few decades ago.

Yet one question remains open:

is more information actually producing better decisions?

Not necessarily.

Because between the data and the decision lies something no dashboard can eliminate:

judgment.

More data does not necessarily mean more evidence

Data and evidence are frequently treated as synonyms.

They are not.

A datum is a piece of information.

It becomes evidence when it is relevant to the specific decision we are trying to make.

This distinction is fundamental.

An organisation may possess hundreds of metrics relating to a problem and still have very little information that is genuinely useful to the decision at hand.

Consider a board deciding whether to enter a new market.

It can analyse:

market size,

projected growth,

consumer behaviour,

competitor activity,

margins,

entry costs,

macroeconomic scenario,

performance of comparable markets.

All potentially useful data.

But none of these, in isolation, determines what weight it should carry relative to the others.

That choice requires judgment.

And this leads to an important distinction:

More Data ≠ Better Evidence ≠ Better Judgment ≠ Better Decision

Every step requires a transformation.

Information must be selected.

Interpreted.

Contextualised.

Weighted.

Compared against other evidence.

And finally applied within a decision.

It is precisely in these transitions that a significant portion of decision risk originates.

The paradox of information abundance

When information was scarce, the problem was relatively clear:

we do not know enough.

When information becomes abundant, a more difficult problem emerges:

we no longer know what deserves attention.

More information means more signals.

But also more noise.

More scenarios.

More interpretations.

More correlations.

More opportunities to find something that confirms what we already believe.

An executive may walk into a meeting with fifty slides of analysis and leave feeling more confident.

But greater confidence does not necessarily mean greater decision quality.

The volume of information can create a sense of control that reality does not justify.

An uncertain decision can appear precise simply because it is accompanied by precise numbers.

The precision of an analysis can be mistaken for the quality of a decision. But a model can be extremely precise at answering the wrong question.

This is one of the paradoxes of modern decision-making:

the precision of an analysis can be mistaken for the quality of a decision.

But a model can be extremely precise at answering the wrong question.

When numbers become an anchor

Numbers carry a particular authority in organisations.

A forecast of 17.4% appears more rigorous than a projection of 15–20%.

A ranking seems more objective than a discussion.

A score appears more reliable than a judgment.

A probability seems to transform uncertainty into something measurable.

But the presence of a number does not eliminate the assumptions that produced it.

Every model contains choices.

Which variables to include.

Which to exclude.

Which historical period to use.

Which behaviour to treat as relevant.

Which outcome to measure.

What weight to assign to each element.

The risk arises when the model's output is examined while the assumptions that generated it disappear from view.

At that point, the number can become an anchor.

The discussion no longer begins from:

"What do we actually know?"

but from:

"Why should we question this number?"

It is a subtle difference.

But it changes the entire architecture of the decision.

Confirmation bias in the age of data

One of the most dangerous assumptions is that more data automatically reduces bias.

It can.

But it can also make bias more sophisticated.

When sufficient information exists, it frequently becomes possible to find evidence compatible with almost any hypothesis.

A leader who believes a market is promising will find data on growth.

A leader who is sceptical will find data on risks.

Those who want to acquire a company can focus on synergies.

Those who are opposed can focus on precedent failures.

Those who consider an executive candidate exceptional can find elements that confirm that perception.

Those who have already developed reservations can interpret the same elements very differently.

The problem, therefore, is not only:

do we have enough data?

It is also:

how are we choosing which data to treat as important?

Confirmation bias does not require an absence of information. It can thrive precisely in its abundance.

Because confirmation bias does not require an absence of information.

It can thrive precisely in its abundance.

The illusion of data-driven decision-making

Being data-driven has become an almost universal objective.

Understandably so.

Organisations should use better evidence.

They should reduce arbitrariness.

They should measure what can be measured.

But there is a critical difference between:

Key distinction
Data-Informed

Data informs a decision. It illuminates context, reduces uncertainty, improves the quality of assumptions. Executive judgment remains determinative.

Data-Determined

The data output replaces judgment. The analysis decides autonomously. Variables that cannot be measured disappear from the conversation.

Data can inform a decision.

It cannot independently determine what an organisation should value.

An algorithm can estimate which candidate possesses certain characteristics.

It cannot decide which characteristics are strategically most important for that specific organisation.

A financial model can calculate multiple scenarios.

It cannot determine what level of risk a board should consider acceptable.

An analytics system can identify a pattern.

It cannot establish whether that pattern will remain valid in a context that is changing.

These steps require judgment.

The true objective, therefore, should not be to create organisations driven by data.

It should be to create organisations capable of using data to improve judgment.

Artificial intelligence will amplify the problem

AI is radically changing the relationship between leaders and information.

An executive can today ask a system to:

analyse thousands of documents,

compare competitors,

identify patterns,

generate scenarios,

summarise markets,

evaluate alternatives,

produce forecasts,

highlight anomalies.

This represents an enormous increase in analytical capacity.

But it also creates a new asymmetry.

Our capacity to generate analysis is growing far more rapidly than our capacity to assess its relevance.

In the future, the challenge may not be obtaining an answer.

It may be choosing among a hundred plausible answers.

AI can reduce the cost of analysis. It does not eliminate the cost of judgment. If anything, it may make judgment more important than ever.

AI can reduce the cost of analysis.

It does not eliminate the cost of judgment.

If anything, it may make judgment more important than ever.

Because when producing an analysis becomes almost free, value shifts towards the capacity to formulate the right question.

The question comes before the analysis

Consider two executives working with exactly the same data.

The first asks:

Same dataset — different questions — different decisions
Executive A

"Which candidate has the strongest track record?"

"Which market shows the highest projected growth?"

Executive B

"Which candidate has the decision-making pattern best suited to the conditions this role will face?"

"In which market do our capabilities create an advantage that competitors will find difficult to replicate?"

Same data.

Potentially very different decisions.

The same data can produce entirely different analyses because the question determines what we consider relevant.

This is why the quality of inquiry precedes the quality of analysis.

A sophisticated answer to the wrong question remains an answer to the wrong question.

The risk of measuring what is easy instead of what matters

Organisations naturally tend to favour what can be measured.

Revenue.

Margin.

Market share.

Time.

Cost.

Historical performance.

Number of clients.

Engagement.

Conversion rate.

These are important metrics.

But some of the most consequential variables in executive decisions are far more difficult to reduce to a number.

Consequential variables — difficult to measure
Quality of judgment
Capacity under ambiguity
Response to pressure
Willingness to update positions
Quality of assumptions
Risk awareness
Signal versus noise distinction

When something is difficult to measure, it risks receiving less attention.

Not because it is less important.

But because it is less easily represented on a dashboard.

Measurability can therefore shape the perception of importance.

And this is precisely where executive judgment becomes indispensable.

Executive hiring: when more assessment creates more confidence

Executive Hiring offers a particularly clear illustration.

To reduce risk, an organisation may add:

more interviews,

more assessments,

more references,

more stakeholders,

more scores,

more career data,

more behavioural analysis.

All of this can be useful.

But there is a prior question:

are we collecting more information about the right decision?

If the role has been poorly defined, more data about the candidate does not resolve the problem.

It may simply increase the confidence with which the organisation makes a decision built on a flawed assumption.

Before increasing the quantity of information about the candidate, it is necessary to increase the quality of how the problem itself is defined.

This is where the Role First principle becomes critical.

Before increasing the quantity of information about the candidate, it is necessary to increase the quality of how the problem itself is defined.

Five questions before asking for more data

When an executive team feels it needs more information, it can be valuable to pause and ask:

1 of 5
What decision are we actually trying to make?

It seems obvious.

It frequently is not.

Defining the decision precisely reduces the risk of gathering information simply because it is available.

2 of 5
What information could actually change our decision?

If a new piece of data could not alter the conclusion, it may not be decisional information.

It is simply information.

3 of 5
Which assumptions are we treating as facts?

Every important decision contains hypotheses.

Making them visible reveals where to look for evidence.

4 of 5
Are we seeking evidence or confirmation?

The distinction is fundamental.

Evidence can change a decision.

Confirmation generally serves only to defend it.

5 of 5
What can the data not tell us?

Every piece of information has limits.

Understanding those limits is an integral part of judgment.

These questions do not reduce the value of data.

They increase it.

Because they help distinguish available information from information that is genuinely useful to the decision.

From data intelligence to decision intelligence

In recent years, organisations have invested enormously in the capacity to collect and analyse information.

The next competitive advantage may derive from something different:

the capacity to understand how that information enters decisions.

This is the territory of Decision Intelligence.

Not the replacement of human judgment.

But making it more observable.

The questions of Decision Intelligence
→ Which information influenced the decision?
→ Which assumptions were present?
→ Which evidence was treated as most important?
→ Which alternatives were excluded?
→ Which uncertainties remain?
→ What could change our position?

These questions shift attention from the volume of information to the quality of the process through which it becomes a decision.

Competitive advantage will not come from having more information

Over the next decade, access to information will become an increasingly weak differentiator.

Many organisations will have access to the same models.

The same datasets.

The same platforms.

The same Artificial Intelligence systems.

The same analytical capabilities.

The difference will progressively be less about:

who possesses more information?

And increasingly about:

who can formulate better questions, distinguish evidence from noise, and apply better judgment?

The leader of the future may not be the one with the most answers. It may be the one who knows which question must be asked before an answer becomes a decision.

This profoundly changes the meaning of leadership.

The leader of the future may not be the one with the most answers.

It may be the one who knows which question must be asked before an answer becomes a decision.

Before the data, the decision

Organisations will continue to accumulate more data.

More analytics.

More AI.

More forecasts.

Greater capacity to simulate the future.

This is an extraordinary advance.

But no quantity of information can entirely eliminate uncertainty from executive decisions.

Because a decision always concerns something that has not yet happened.

And that is precisely where judgment enters.

The question, therefore, is not:

"How can we have more data?"

It is:

"How can we make better use of what we know to reach a better decision?"

This distinction separates Information Intelligence from Decision Intelligence.

And it may prove to be one of the most important organisational capabilities of the age of Artificial Intelligence.
