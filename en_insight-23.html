The risk of a decision also depends
on the ability to reverse it.

Before making a strategic commitment, organisations evaluate opportunities, risks and expected results. Far less often do they examine how difficult it will be to modify, interrupt or abandon the choice. Yet the cost of reversing course can become one of the most important dimensions of decision risk.

Before making a strategic commitment, organisations evaluate opportunities, risks and expected results. Far less often do they examine how difficult it will be to modify, interrupt or abandon the choice. Yet the cost of reversing course can become one of the most important dimensions of decision risk.

A good decision can become a commitment that is hard to change

A board of directors approves a multi-year investment.

A chief executive authorises a transformation.

An industrial group chooses a new strategic partner.

The analyses are convincing. The alternatives have been compared. The risk appears compatible with the objectives.

The decision is approved.

But during execution the context changes.

Costs increase. Some assumptions lose validity. The initial opportunities shrink.

The organisation recognises the change.

And yet continuing seems simpler than stopping.

Resources have already been committed. Contracts have been signed. Expectations have been communicated. Interrupting the path would involve economic and organisational costs.

The initial decision may have been rational.

But the commitment taken on may have significantly reduced the freedom to change it.

RATIO Principle
A decision does not become safer because we can change our minds.
It becomes more governable when we know the cost of doing so.

Reversibility is not an absolute condition

Decisions are often described as reversible or irreversible.

But in executive reality this distinction is rarely binary.

Some choices can be modified at limited cost.

Others can be interrupted only by sacrificing a significant part of the investment.

Others still produce contractual, organisational or reputational consequences that cannot be fully undone.

We distinguish three conditions.

RATIO — Three Conditions of Reversibility
01
Highly Reversible Decision
The choice can be modified or interrupted at relatively limited cost and consequence.
02
Partially Reversible Decision
It is possible to change direction, but some commitments, resources or consequences cannot be recovered.
03
Hardly Reversible Decision
Modifying or interrupting the choice involves high costs, significant constraints or consequences that are difficult to offset.
REVERSIBILITY ≠ ABSENCE OF COSTS

The question is not only whether we can reverse course.

It is how much of what we have committed we will actually be able to recover or preserve.

Commitment exposure

A strategic decision does not produce only an investment.

It produces a set of commitments.

Some are financial.

Others are contractual, organisational or reputational.

We define commitment exposure as the set of consequences that make it progressively more costly to modify or interrupt a decision.

RATIO — Commitment Exposure
01
Financial
Capital already invested and further economic obligations.
02
Contractual
Commitments, penalties, constraints and obligations towards third parties.
03
Organisational
Structures, competencies and processes developed around the choice.
04
Reputational
Expectations created among investors, employees, customers and partners.
05
Strategic
Alternatives that become less accessible because of the commitment taken on.

These dimensions do not necessarily carry the same weight.

But they can interact and amplify the overall cost of change.

RATIO Principle
The cost of a decision does not always coincide with the cost of making it.
It can also include the cost of changing it.

Irreversibility can increase over time

An initially flexible decision can progressively become rigid.

A preliminary agreement becomes a definitive contract.

A pilot becomes a permanent infrastructure.

A project involves new functions.

People are hired.

Systems are integrated.

Previous alternatives are abandoned.

Every step can increase the cost of change.

Initial Decision

This progression is not necessarily negative.

Every important strategy requires a certain level of commitment.

The risk emerges when the organisation increases exposure without recognising that it is reducing its own flexibility.

"The reversibility of a decision can decrease
even when the decision itself does not change."

Designing reversibility before the commitment

Organisations often start discussing how to exit a decision only once difficulties emerge.

But some reversibility conditions can be considered beforehand.

We define designed reversibility as the discipline of assessing, while preparing the decision, which elements can preserve a reasonable capacity to adapt.

Four conceptual tools are particularly relevant.

RATIO — Designed Reversibility
01
Progressive Commitments
Break down exposure into phases when the nature of the decision allows it.
02
Activation Conditions
Link new commitments to the achievement of defined conditions.
03
Contractual and Organisational Flexibility
Where possible, evaluate alternatives that reduce unnecessary constraints.
04
Checkpoints
Establish moments at which new evidence can justify continuing, adapting or interrupting the path.
RATIO Principle
Designing reversibility does not mean preparing to fail.
It means preserving the ability to respond to reality.

Not every decision allows for these solutions.

But their feasibility should be examined before taking on commitments that are hard to modify.

The RATIO Exit Conditions Model

We introduce a new framework: the RATIO Exit Conditions Model.

Its purpose is to make explicit the conditions under which a strategic commitment should be re-evaluated and the concrete possibilities for modifying it.

RATIO — Exit Conditions Model
01
Commitment
What are we actually taking on as an obligation or strategic choice?
02
Exposure
Which costs and consequences will increase if we continue?
03
Flexibility
Which alternatives will remain available after activation?
04
Signals
Which changes could materially call the initial logic into question?
05
Exit Conditions
Under what circumstances should we evaluate modifying, suspending or interrupting the choice?
06
Authority
Who holds the mandate to evaluate these conditions and authorise any changes?

The model does not introduce automatic decisions.

It serves to prevent exit possibilities from being considered only once the cost of change has already become high.

An exit condition is not an automatic order

Defining certain conditions in advance does not mean establishing that every signal must trigger an interruption.

A rise in costs may call for a review.

A drop in demand may justify an adaptation.

A regulatory change may make a reassessment necessary.

But none of these events automatically determines what the best choice is.

A distinction must be drawn:

RATIO — Three Levels of Response
01
Attention Signal
A change deserves to be examined.
02
Reassessment Condition
A material variation requires a new evaluation of the decision.
03
Exit Decision
The competent authority concludes that interrupting or modifying the commitment is preferable to continuing, in compliance with applicable obligations.
RATIO Principle
A signal is not a decision.
An exit condition does not replace judgement.

The goal is to improve the timing and quality of the evaluation, not to automate the abandonment of initiatives.

Sunk cost should not govern the future

One of the most significant risks arises when resources already deployed begin to dominate judgement.

"We've already invested too much to stop now."

"We can't abandon the project after two years."

"It would be hard to explain giving up."

These statements can describe real difficulties.

But they do not prove that continuing is the correct choice.

Resources already spent and unrecoverable belong to the past.

The current decision should compare the future consequences of the available alternatives, including costs that are still avoidable and those of a possible exit.

RATIO — Two Different Questions
01
How Much Have We Already Invested?
Describes the historical commitment.
02
Which Choice Produces the Best Balance Today?
Between expected value, risk and future costs. Guides current judgement.

"Money already spent is not, by itself,
a reason to spend more."

This does not mean ignoring the consequences of interruption.

It means preventing past investment from becoming an automatic justification for continuing.

An executive scenario: the global technology platform

An international group decides to replace its operating systems with a common technology platform.

The project promises greater integration, data consistency and efficiency.

The board of directors approves the investment.

Management launches the transformation.

During the first year some critical issues emerge.

Integration costs turn out to be higher than forecast.

Some functions require complex customisations.

Part of the expected benefits slips over time.

The project remains potentially valid, but exposure keeps increasing.

Had the organisation planned from the outset for autonomous phases, interim checkpoints and appropriate contractual conditions, it might have a greater capacity to adapt.

It could confirm some components.

Scale down others.

Renegotiate certain commitments.

Or reassess part of the initiative before extending it to the entire group.

The possibility of changing course does not prove the initial decision was wrong.

It demonstrates the value of having considered the conditions of change in advance.

Exiting also has a cost

The principle of reversibility should not be interpreted as an invitation to abandon decisions as soon as difficulties arise.

Interrupting can generate significant costs.

Contractual penalties.

Loss of future benefits.

Damage to commercial relationships.

Operational disruptions.

Transition costs.

Consequences for people.

The available alternatives must therefore be compared.

RATIO — Three Possibilities
01
Continue
When the expected value of continuing remains sufficiently solid relative to future risks and costs.
02
Adapt
When modifying timing, scale or structure of the commitment can preserve value and reduce vulnerability.
03
Interrupt
When continuing no longer appears justified relative to the alternatives, considering exit costs, obligations and consequences.
RATIO Principle
The possibility of exiting does not always make it advantageous to do so.

A decision can be hard to interrupt and still remain rational.

The point is not to systematically favour flexibility.

It is to understand the cost of the alternatives before it is too late to evaluate them.

The value of future options

In Insight #22 we addressed strategic trade-offs.

Every choice involves giving something up.

Some commitments reduce the number of future alternatives.

Others can preserve them.

Reversibility adds an important dimension to the evaluation.

Two initiatives with similar expected results might have very different levels of flexibility.

One requires an immediate and hardly recoverable investment.

The other allows a progressive path, with the possibility of revisiting the commitment after new evidence.

The second is not automatically better.

But the ability to preserve future options can hold strategic value, especially under conditions of high uncertainty.

EXPECTED VALUE ≠ EXPECTED VALUE CONSIDERING FLEXIBILITY

The ability to change direction is a resource.

But it must be weighed against its costs.

Preserving every option indefinitely can prevent building any significant competitive advantage.

Reversibility and time

In Insight #16 we analysed the cost of delay.

Some decisions need time to reduce uncertainty.

Others lose value when postponed.

Reversibility contributes to this balance.

An easily modifiable decision can, in some circumstances, allow for a limited initial commitment and progressive learning.

A hardly reversible decision may require more rigorous verification before activation.

But not always.

When waiting also carries high costs, the organisation must simultaneously evaluate the risk of committing and the risk of delaying.

RATIO Principle
Reversibility influences how we decide.
It does not automatically determine when we must decide.

Judgement requires considering time, uncertainty, alternatives and consequences jointly.

People decisions also create commitments

Reversibility also concerns executive selection.

An important appointment produces organisational consequences.

It changes responsibilities.

It creates expectations.

It can influence management behaviour and strategic priorities.

An organisation should not interpret the appointment of an executive as a simple, costless reversible choice.

At the same time, it should not consider it immutable.

The quality of the decision requires upfront clarity on the role, the mandate, the organisational conditions and the criteria for subsequent evaluation.

Any changes must respect contractual obligations, professional fairness, applicable protections and responsibility towards the people involved.

This reinforces the RATIO principle:

RATIO Principle
Role First.
Then the Candidate.

An appointment does not become more solid because it is hard to change.

It becomes more governable when the role, the mandate and the conditions under which it will be evaluated are sufficiently clear.

Artificial intelligence can simulate the exit. Not guarantee its cost.

Artificial intelligence can help compare scenarios.

It can simulate the costs of continuing and interrupting.

It can identify contractual and organisational dependencies present in the available information.

It can help represent alternative paths.

But a simulation is not equivalent to complete knowledge of the consequences.

Some costs can be difficult to quantify.

Relationships between people can alter the real effects.

Contractual constraints require appropriate interpretation.

Reputation and trust cannot be fully represented by a numerical model.

SIMULATED EXIT ≠ ACTUALLY FEASIBLE EXIT

Artificial intelligence can improve the visibility of alternatives.

It does not replace the concrete verification of constraints or the judgement of those who hold decision-making authority.

RATIO operates in the territory of executive decision intelligence. Its focus concerns the quality of the judgement that precedes strategic choices.

Reversibility introduces a further question: how difficult will it be to modify this decision if the conditions on which it rests were to change?

RATIO does not replace the board of directors. It does not decide when to interrupt an investment. It does not guarantee the ability to recover committed resources. Its contribution remains in the quality of the decision-making process: making consequences, constraints and possibilities more visible before the organisation takes on significant commitments.

The final principle

Organisations must take on commitments.

Without commitment there is no realised strategy.

There is no transformation.

There is no growth.

But not every commitment has the same reversibility.

Some preserve flexibility.

Others reduce it quickly.

Some can be modified at limited cost.

Others produce consequences that are hardly recoverable.

Executive judgement does not consist in systematically avoiding irreversible decisions.

It consists in recognising them, understanding their consequences and taking on commitments proportionate to the expected value, the risk and the available alternatives.

RATIO Principle #23
Every decision creates a commitment.
Every commitment can reduce alternatives.
Knowing the cost of reversing course improves judgement before moving forward.

The quality of a decision does not depend only on what we hope to achieve.

It also depends on awareness of the consequences we accept if reality were to change.

Question for the Board
Think of an important strategic decision your organisation is about to approve.

You probably know the required investment. You have evaluated risks and expected results. You have compared alternatives.

But do you know how much it would cost to interrupt it? Which resources would not be recoverable? Which obligations would continue to exist? Which alternatives would become less accessible?

And above all: if in twelve months the fundamental assumptions were no longer valid, would you still have a concrete possibility of changing direction without disproportionately compromising the organisation?

If the answer is not clear, there may be a risk that the initial evaluation has not yet made sufficiently visible.

RATIO — Decision Intelligence

Organisations cannot eliminate uncertainty. But they can improve the way they take on commitments and make visible the costs of change before reversing course becomes difficult.

RATIO makes visible the exposure, the exit conditions and the cost of modifying strategic decisions, distinguishing a conscious commitment from an unrecognised rigidity.

Decision quality does not consist in avoiding difficult commitments. It consists in taking them on knowing, as far as possible, the consequences of the choice.
