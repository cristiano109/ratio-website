When Experience Becomes a Decision Risk

Why the most experienced leaders can become the most vulnerable when context changes

Experience recognises the past. Judgment must determine whether it is still relevant.

Experience is one of the most valuable assets in leadership. But the very capability that makes it powerful contains a risk that organisations rarely examine with sufficient rigour.

Experience is one of the most valuable assets in leadership.

It allows patterns to be recognised.

It reduces the time required to understand a situation.

It helps distinguish what matters from what can be safely ignored.

It enables years of prior decisions to be used to rapidly interpret what is happening today.

But this very capability contains a risk.

Experience can accelerate a decision before we have verified whether the problem we are confronting is truly the same one we have already encountered.

And this is where an advantage can become a vulnerability.

Experience does not see the future. It recognises the past.

When an executive faces a complex situation, they rarely start from scratch.

They unconsciously compare what they are observing with thousands of situations already encountered.

A crisis.

A transformation.

An executive appointment.

An acquisition.

A new market.

An organisational conflict.

A margin compression.

The mind searches for something familiar.

"I have seen this before."

This statement can be extremely useful.

But it contains a question that is rarely asked:

how closely does this situation actually resemble the one we believe we remember?

Because recognising a similarity does not mean having identified the same problem.

The expertise paradox

The more experience we accumulate, the larger our pattern library becomes.

This makes it possible to make decisions very quickly.

But it also increases the number of analogies we can use to interpret a novel situation.

The problem is not having experience.

The problem arises when experience stops being evidence and becomes a premise.

"It worked then, so it will probably work again."

This conclusion may be correct.

Or it may be one of the most costly assumptions embedded in a decision.

Because between "then" and "now" the following may have changed:

What may have changed between "then" and "now"
The market
Technology
The people
Organisational culture
Competitive velocity
Capital availability
Customer expectations
The power structure
The level of uncertainty

The decision may appear familiar.

The context may not be at all.

Competence produces speed. Not necessarily accuracy.

An experienced executive can grasp in minutes what a less experienced manager requires hours to understand.

This speed has enormous value.

But speed and decision quality are not synonyms.

Speed often derives from the ability to compress complexity.

The leader rapidly identifies:

"this is an execution problem";

"this is a leadership problem";

"this person is not the right fit";

"this market will not work";

"this transformation requires a different profile".

Each definition drastically reduces the space of alternatives.

And that is precisely the point.

Before even selecting a solution, we have already chosen which problem we believe we are facing.

If that definition is correct, experience accelerates the decision enormously.

If it is wrong, experience can accelerate in the wrong direction.

The risk of decisional compression

Expertise allows vast amounts of information to be compressed into synthetic judgements.

"Something about this doesn't feel right."

"That is the right profile."

"I know this market."

"This strategy will not work."

"We need someone more commercial."

Behind each statement there may be twenty years of experience.

And that is precisely why they are rarely challenged.

But an important decision should be able to distinguish at least three levels:

RATIO — DECISION FRAMEWORK · THREE LEVELS
WHAT WE KNOW
Verified facts, observable data, structured evidence
↓
WHAT WE INFER
Conclusions drawn from interpreting available data
↓
WHAT WE ASSUME
Unverified premises that silently shape judgement

When these three levels are compressed into a single perception, it becomes difficult to understand where the judgement actually originates.

And even more difficult to test it.

The authority of experience

There is also a second dynamic.

Experience does not only influence the decision-maker.

It influences everyone who participates in the decision.

Imagine a CEO with thirty years of industry experience saying during a meeting:

"I know this type of situation. We need to do X."

From that moment, the problem changes.

The team is no longer simply analysing a decision.

They are evaluating whether to challenge the CEO's experience.

The conversation can then shift — unconsciously — from searching for the best solution to searching for the evidence that supports the initial interpretation.

Experience becomes an anchor.

And the organisation begins to build the decision around it.

When past success becomes particularly dangerous

Not all experiences carry equal weight.

Experiences associated with significant successes become particularly powerful.

A leader who has transformed a business.

Built a market.

Executed a remarkable acquisition.

Scaled an organisation.

Navigated a crisis.

Naturally develops convictions about what produced that result.

But success carries an epistemological problem:

we can rarely know with precision how much of the outcome was produced by our decisions and how much by the context in which those decisions were made.

Contextual factors that contribute to success
Often attributed to the decision

The chosen strategy. The leader's profile. The execution model. The priorities defined. The process adopted.

Often not considered

Favourable market conditions. Timing. Available capital. Weak competitors. Exceptional team. Emerging technology. Luck.

A strategy may have been excellent.

But transforming a successful strategy into a universal law is an entirely different step.

Success leaves traces. But it can also leave illusions.

Organisations often study their own successes.

"What did we do well?"

This is a useful question.

But it may not be sufficient.

A harder question is:

"What conditions allowed that decision to work?"

The distinction is enormous.

Because we can replicate a decision.

We cannot necessarily replicate the context that made it effective.

EXPERIENCE ≠ REPEATABILITY
PAST SUCCESS ≠ FUTURE EVIDENCE
Context is not replicable — decisions cannot be automatically transferred

Experience becomes genuinely useful when it enables us to formulate better hypotheses.

It becomes dangerous when it enables us to stop formulating them.

When context changes faster than experience

For much of management history, many organisational patterns changed slowly.

Today several elements can transform simultaneously:

Elements transforming simultaneously
Technology and AI
Business models
People expectations
Regulation
Geopolitics
Markets and competition
Speed of information

This produces an important consequence.

The duration of experience increases while the validity period of some experiences may decrease.

An executive may have thirty years of experience.

But the decisive question is not:

"How many years of experience do they have?"

It is:

"What part of that experience remains relevant to the problem we need to address today?"

Executive Hiring: where the past weighs most

Executive Hiring makes this problem particularly visible.

When we evaluate a senior candidate, we inevitably look at the past.

Roles held.

Organisations.

Results.

Sectors.

Size of organisations managed.

Transformations delivered.

P&L managed.

Teams led.

All correct.

But there is a logical leap that can become dangerous:

they succeeded there → they will succeed here.

Not necessarily.

The most important question is not only about what the candidate has done.

It concerns the conditions under which they did it.

The questions that precede the track record
→ How much authority did they hold in that role?
→ What organisational culture surrounded them?
→ How strong was the team?
→ What was the state of the market?
→ How much support did they receive from the CEO?
→ What level of autonomy did they have?
→ What decisions did they actually have to make?

The track record belongs to the candidate. But performance always belongs to the interaction between person and context.

Role First: the problem comes before the CV

This is where the Role First principle emerges.

Before asking:

"Who is the best candidate?"

we should understand:

"What reality will this person need to navigate?"

Which decisions will be determinative?

What organisational tensions will they encounter?

What ambiguity will they need to manage?

Which stakeholders will shape the role?

What type of judgement will be required?

Which behaviours worked in the past but may no longer be effective?

Only then can we observe a candidate's experience with a different question.

Not:

"How impressive is it?"

But:

"How transferable is it?"

Five questions to test experience

1 of 5
What part of the situation are we recognising?

Defining the analogy makes it possible to test it.

A feeling of familiarity is not yet evidence.

2 of 5
What has changed since the last time?

Market, people, technology, governance, and timing can completely transform what appears to be the same decision.

3 of 5
Which of our convictions derives from past success?

Successes create powerful mental models.

Making them explicit allows us to determine whether they remain valid.

4 of 5
What evidence would cause us to change our position?

If no information could modify the conclusion, we are probably no longer evaluating a hypothesis.

We are defending a conviction.

5 of 5
Are we using experience to formulate better questions — or to avoid formulating them?

This may be the most important distinction.

Experience should increase the quality of inquiry.

Not eliminate its necessity.

From experience-driven to judgment-driven

The solution is not to diminish the value of experience.

It is to use it differently.

Experience is an extraordinary source of patterns.

Of hypotheses.

Of signals.

Of questions.

Of scenarios.

But decisions require something more:

judgement.

Judgement allows us to ask whether what we learned in the past remains applicable to the present.

It is the ability to hold two ideas simultaneously:

"I have seen something similar before."

and

"I may never have seen exactly this."

It is in the space between these two statements that an important part of Decision Intelligence is born.

The advantage of the best leaders will not be knowing more

In a world where AI is making information, analysis, benchmarks, and scenarios increasingly accessible, even informational experience will become less exclusive.

The advantage may shift.

From the quantity of what a leader knows to the quality with which they interrogate what they believe they know.

From pattern recognition to the ability to recognise when a pattern is no longer valid.

From the confidence derived from experience to the discipline of subjecting it to scrutiny.

This does not make experience less important.

It makes it more sophisticated.

The most experienced leader is not necessarily the one who has seen the most situations. It may be the one who knows how to recognise when what they have already seen is no longer sufficient to decide what lies before them.

Before experience, context

Experience will continue to hold enormous value in organisations.

But it should not become a shortcut to certainty.

Because every important decision contains something the past cannot fully know:

the present context.

The question therefore is not:

"Do we have enough experience to make this decision?"

It is:

"Which part of our experience is genuinely relevant to the decision we need to make today?"

Board Question
In your organisation's last major strategic decision,
how many of the initial convictions derived from evidence about the current context —
and how many from what had worked in the past?

If we cannot distinguish between them, experience may be influencing the decision more than we realise.

The ability to separate accumulated experience from contextual relevance may be one of the most differentiating capabilities in executive leadership over the coming years.
