Executive judgement does not consist only in recognising opportunities. It also consists in knowing which ones to give up.

Not every good opportunity is a good decision

A company receives an attractive acquisition proposal.

The price appears reasonable. The growth prospects are convincing. The synergies seem achievable.

The board of directors analyses the deal.

The results of the evaluation are positive.

And yet it decides not to proceed.

Not because the opportunity is weak.

Not because the risks are excessive.

But because pursuing it would require resources and attention the organisation has already committed to more important strategic priorities.

The choice may appear counterintuitive.

We are used to measuring decision quality through what gets approved, realised and obtained.

Far less often do we consider the value of the opportunities an organisation consciously chooses to give up.

And yet this too is a decision-making capability.

RATIO Principle
An opportunity can be valid
without being the right choice for the organisation.

The quality of judgement does not consist only in recognising what might work.

It consists in understanding what truly deserves to be pursued.

Not deciding is different from deciding not to act

In Insight #16 we addressed the cost of decision delay.

An organisation can keep analysing, postponing and waiting, without recognising that time itself changes the alternatives available.

But an essential distinction exists.

Not deciding means leaving a choice unresolved, consciously or not.

Deciding not to proceed means evaluating an opportunity and concluding that it should not be pursued under current conditions.

The first behaviour can stem from indecision.

The second can represent a rigorous exercise of judgement.

RATIO — Three Distinct Conditions
01
Indecision
The question remains open without a sufficiently defined conclusion.
02
Justified Postponement
The decision is postponed because specific information or conditions could materially change its evaluation.
03
Conscious Renunciation
The organisation concludes that the opportunity should not be pursued, based on explicit criteria and priorities.
NOT DECIDING ≠ DECIDING NOT TO ACT

Giving something up is not necessarily a sign of weakness.

It can demonstrate that the organisation knows the limits of its own strategic capacity.

Every yes contains a no

Organisational resources are limited.

Capital is limited.

Specialist competencies are limited.

Management time is limited.

Even the capacity to handle change across multiple initiatives at once has limits.

When an organisation approves a new project, it is not only adding an activity.

It is also committing capacity that will no longer be available in the same way elsewhere.

A yes can therefore contain several implicit no's.

A yes to a new acquisition can mean less financial capacity for an internal transformation.

A yes to an expansion can mean less management attention to consolidate an existing market.

A yes to a new initiative can mean delaying a project already approved.

This does not make the yes wrong.

It makes it necessary to understand its cost.

RATIO Principle
Every strategic commitment uses capacity
that will no longer be available for all other alternatives.

The question is not only:

"Can this opportunity create value?"

It is also:

"What are we giving up in order to pursue it?"

The invisible cost of accepted opportunities

An opportunity is often evaluated through its own expected return.

How much can it generate? How much capital does it require? What risks does it carry? What competitive advantage can it offer?

These are necessary questions.

But they can be insufficient when considered in isolation.

A decision can appear advantageous without being the best possible destination for the available resources.

The cost of a choice also includes the value of the alternatives we give up.

In economics this principle is known as opportunity cost.

In executive judgement it takes on a broader dimension.

It does not concern money alone.

It concerns the organisation's overall strategic capacity.

RATIO — Three Costs to Distinguish
01
Direct Cost
The resources required to realise the initiative.
02
Capacity Cost
The attention, competencies and organisational availability absorbed.
03
Cost of Alternatives
The potential value of the options that could not be pursued, or that must be postponed or scaled down.
VALUE OF THE OPPORTUNITY ≠ VALUE OF THE CHOICE

A complete evaluation must consider not only what the initiative promises, but also what pursuing it makes less possible.

The connection to the decision portfolio

In Insight #21 we introduced decision portfolio risk.

Multiple individually rational decisions can build a fragile overall exposure.

They can depend on the same assumptions.

Compete for the same resources.

Concentrate in the same period.

The new theme represents the next step.

If every decision modifies the organisation's portfolio, then the quality of a choice cannot be evaluated independently of the commitments already made.

A new opportunity must be considered against existing priorities.

It is not enough to ask whether it is advantageous.

It is necessary to ask whether it improves the overall portfolio or makes it less sustainable.

New Strategic Trade-offs

The risk does not consist only in approving the wrong initiatives.

It can consist in approving too many, even when each one has a reasonable rationale.

A strategic trade-off is not a failure

Organisations can interpret renunciation as a loss.

A project not realised. A market not conquered. An acquisition not completed. An opportunity left to a competitor.

But the value of a choice does not coincide with the number of initiatives approved.

A strategy requires focus.

And focus necessarily implies exclusions.

We define a conscious strategic trade-off as the explicit choice to favour certain opportunities while giving up others, based on priorities, capacity, risk and relative value.

This does not mean arguing that doing less is always better.

Excessive concentration can mean losing important opportunities.

An overly cautious organisation can compromise its own future capacity.

The point is not to indiscriminately reduce the number of decisions.

It is to assess whether every new commitment deserves the resources and possibilities it takes away from the alternatives.

RATIO Principle
Strategic discipline does not consist in always saying no.
It consists in knowing when a yes is not justified.

The RATIO Strategic Trade-off Model

We introduce a new conceptual framework: the RATIO Strategic Trade-off Model.

Before taking on a significant commitment, the organisation can assess five dimensions.

RATIO — Strategic Trade-off Model
01
Value
What potential value can the opportunity generate? On what evidence and assumptions is it based?
02
Coherence
How consistent is the opportunity with the organisation's actual strategic priorities?
03
Capacity
What financial, operational and managerial resources does it require? How much is actually available?
04
Trade-off
Which initiatives, alternatives or future possibilities would be sacrificed, delayed or scaled down?
05
Flexibility
How much does the choice reduce or preserve the ability to change direction if the context changes?

The model does not automatically assign a score.

It does not produce a universal answer.

It serves to make explicit a dimension that is often overlooked:

"The value of an opportunity must be weighed
against the value of the alternatives that pursuing it makes less accessible."

An executive scenario: the acquisition that is not made

An international group is completing an industrial transformation.

The strategy involves modernising its plants, digitalising processes and strengthening operational management.

During this process, an acquisition opportunity emerges.

A competing company is available at an attractive valuation.

The deal could increase market share. The commercial synergies appear credible. The economic potential is positive.

The board of directors initiates an evaluation.

The analysis confirms that the acquisition could create value.

But it also surfaces other conditions.

The leadership team is already heavily committed to the transformation. Financial resources are sufficient, but not unlimited. Integrating the acquisition would require the same specialist competencies needed to complete the industrial project.

Moreover, any slowdown in the transformation could compromise benefits already planned and considered priorities.

At this point the question changes.

No longer only:

"Is the acquisition attractive?"

But:

"Is the acquisition sufficiently more important
than the alternatives we would have to sacrifice?"

The board decides not to proceed.

Not because the deal lacks value.

But because the overall strategic cost is incompatible with the organisation's priorities and available capacity.

We cannot know with certainty whether this renunciation will produce the best outcome.

But we can recognise that the reasoning considered the relative value of the choice.

A decision not to proceed can be as rigorous as an investment decision.

Four possible responses to an opportunity

A strategic evaluation does not necessarily have to conclude with an absolute yes or no.

There are at least four responses.

RATIO — Four Possible Responses
01
Proceed
The opportunity is sufficiently coherent, sustainable and prioritised relative to the alternatives.
02
Modify
The opportunity has value, but requires a different scale, structure or distribution of the commitment.
03
Postpone with Conditions
The decision is postponed because specific events or information could change its evaluation. The cost of the delay must be explicitly considered.
04
Decline
The opportunity does not justify, under current conditions, the sacrifice of the resources or alternatives needed to pursue it.
RATIO Principle
Proceeding is not always courage.
Declining is not always prudence.
The value lies in the quality of the distinction.

A conscious decision must have a conclusion and a recognisable logic.

A renunciation should not be judged by the future alone

Imagine that the company in the previous example gives up the acquisition.

Two years later, the competitor acquired by another group achieves extraordinary results.

The renunciation might then be considered a mistake.

But would that necessarily be a correct conclusion?

Not always.

Insight #14 showed that decision quality does not coincide with outcome quality.

The same principle applies to rejected opportunities.

The subsequent success of an opportunity does not automatically prove that pursuing it would have been the right choice for every organisation.

The context, resources, priorities and alternatives can be different.

The correct question is:

"Was the renunciation reasonable based on the information,
capacity and priorities available at the moment of the choice?"

A good renunciation can be followed by a lost opportunity.

Just as a weak investment can be followed by a favourable outcome.

The evaluation must preserve the distinction between judgement and consequences.

The ability to say no must be governed

Giving up an opportunity should not depend only on the personal preferences of an executive.

A proposal can be rejected because it challenges existing interests.

A project can be excluded because it introduces change.

An opportunity can be ignored because it does not fit the management's established beliefs.

These behaviours do not necessarily represent strategic discipline.

They can be forms of resistance or distortion of judgement.

A quality no requires recognisable criteria.

Which alternatives have we compared? Which capacities are genuinely limited? Which priorities are we protecting? What evidence could change the evaluation? Which authority made the decision?

RATIO Principle
A no is not strategic simply because it was decided.
It must be justifiable.

Discipline requires the same quality of reasoning both when approving and when declining.

Artificial intelligence increases opportunities. Not the capacity to pursue them all.

Artificial intelligence can help organisations identify new opportunities.

It can build scenarios. It can propose markets. It can highlight promising initiatives. It can quickly produce economic analyses and strategic comparisons.

This increases exploration capacity.

But it does not eliminate organisational limits.

A company can identify ten interesting initiatives without having the capacity to realise them all.

The ease with which new alternatives are generated can therefore make discipline in selection even more important.

MORE OPPORTUNITIES IDENTIFIED ≠ MORE CAPACITY AVAILABLE

The value of artificial intelligence does not depend only on the ability to propose new possibilities.

It also depends on the quality of the human judgement with which those possibilities are compared, selected or excluded.

People decisions also require trade-offs

The principle also applies to executive selection.

An organisation can identify a highly experienced executive.

The candidate has significant achievements. The competencies are recognised. The reputation is solid.

But the role might require different characteristics.

Or the organisation might not be ready to support the mandate.

Or the hire might introduce a commitment inconsistent with other priorities.

In these cases, deciding not to proceed does not necessarily equate to rejecting the value of the person.

It means recognising that a candidate's value is not independent of the role and the context.

It is the RATIO principle:

RATIO Principle
Role First.
Then the Candidate.
AN EXCELLENT CANDIDATE IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY THE RIGHT CHOICE FOR EVERY ROLE

The quality of a hiring decision can also manifest in the ability to give up a convincing candidacy when sufficient decision coherence is not present.

RATIO operates in the territory of executive decision intelligence. Its focus concerns the quality of the reasoning that underpins strategic choices.

The discipline of strategic trade-offs adds a question: does the opportunity we are evaluating truly deserve the resources, attention and alternatives we would have to give up in order to pursue it?

RATIO does not autonomously decide which strategies should be approved. It does not replace the judgement of executives. It does not guarantee the future value of accepted or rejected opportunities. It helps make more visible the conditions that allow different choices to be compared with greater awareness. Its objective remains protecting the quality of the decision-making process.

The final principle

Organisations are not defined only by the decisions they make.

They are also defined by the possibilities they choose not to pursue.

Every investment changes the resources available. Every new priority changes the weight of the others. Every additional initiative can increase the organisation's value, but it can also reduce its ability to concentrate.

Decision maturity does not consist in recognising the greatest possible number of opportunities.

It consists in distinguishing those that truly deserve a commitment.

Saying yes can require courage. Saying no can require discipline. Both choices require judgement.

RATIO Principle #22
Not every good opportunity deserves a yes.
Not every renunciation is a loss.
Strategic discipline protects what truly matters.

Decision quality is not only about what we choose to do.

It is also about what we consciously choose not to do.

Question for the Board
Think of the strategic opportunities currently under evaluation in your organisation.

You probably know their potential. You have analysed returns, risks and prospects.

But do you know how to identify what each opportunity would require you to sacrifice? What resources would it take away from existing priorities? Which alternatives would it make less accessible? What organisational capacity would it commit?

And above all: if you had to approve today every opportunity you consider interesting, which truly important priorities would you risk no longer being able to devote yourselves to?

The answer may reveal a problem that does not emerge from evaluating a single initiative: the risk of not choosing with sufficient discipline.
