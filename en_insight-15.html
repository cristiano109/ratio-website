Changing a Decision Does Not Necessarily Mean It Was Wrong.

A decision can have been sound when it was made and become wrong to maintain when the conditions that made it rational have changed.

A Board approves an acquisition. A CEO commits to a new market. An organisation appoints a senior executive. A leadership team launches a transformation. A strategic investment is sanctioned. In each case, at the moment of decision, evidence is examined, assumptions are made, alternatives are weighed, risks are accepted. The decision is taken. And from that point, something subtle begins.

The organisation stops asking:

"Is this still the right decision?"

And begins asking:

"How do we make this decision work?"

The distinction seems small.

It is not.

The first question keeps the decision open to reality.

The second assumes the decision must remain intact.

Budgets are allocated. People are made accountable. Public commitments are communicated. Reputations attach to the choice. Time passes. And the cost of reconsidering begins to rise.

Not necessarily because the decision is still correct.

But because the organisation has committed to it.

"What if the world that justified that decision has changed?"

A sound decision has a context

No consequential decision is universally correct.

It is correct in relation to:

the information available at the time it was made,

the assumptions considered reasonable,

the objectives being pursued,

the alternatives available,

the constraints in place,

the risks accepted,

the operating context in which the decision must function.

Therefore: decision quality is contextual.

A decision can be solid at T1. The same decision can become inappropriate at T2. This does not automatically mean the organisation made an error at T1. It may mean that reality has changed.

Assumption Half-Life

RATIO Concept
Assumption Half-Life

Every strategic decision contains assumptions. Market demand will remain within a certain range. Capital will be available. An executive will receive the organisational support required. A regulatory environment will stay stable. A competitor will behave predictably. A technology will mature at a given pace. A transformation will achieve internal adoption.

Some assumptions hold for years. Others can become obsolete within weeks. The more volatile the environment, the shorter the useful life of certain assumptions.

Therefore, organisations should not only ask: "What assumptions are we making?" They should also ask: "For how long can we reasonably expect these assumptions to remain valid?"

Consistency is not the same as decision quality

Senior leaders are frequently expected to demonstrate conviction. Once a strategic direction has been communicated, reversing it can feel costly. A CEO who changes course may fear appearing uncertain. A Board may resist reopening a decision it previously approved. A leadership team may defend a transformation because too much political capital has already been invested.

But consistency only holds value as long as the underlying reasoning remains valid.

"Conviction has value.
Attachment to invalidated assumptions does not."

The goal is not to create leaders who constantly change their minds. The goal is to build organisations capable of distinguishing:

Discipline
Maintaining a decision when the conditions that justified it are still valid.
vs
Rigidity
Maintaining a decision to defend past commitment, even when conditions have changed.

Not every new signal justifies a new decision

Markets fluctuate. Performance varies. People react emotionally. The first phase of execution produces noise. One negative quarter does not automatically invalidate a strategy. A difficult first month does not automatically invalidate an executive appointment. A competitor's move does not automatically invalidate a market decision. A single new data point is not necessarily new evidence.

Decision Reassessment therefore requires discipline.

Signal
A variation in the flow of information. Requires interpretation before it becomes relevant.
≠
Evidence
Information that materially changes the logic that supported the original decision.

Equally: a negative outcome is not automatically proof that the decision was wrong — as Insight #14 distinguished with precision. Reassessment must respond to logic, not to emotion.

If none of these conditions exist, short-term negative performance or discomfort does not justify reopening. If one or more conditions exist materially, refusing to reassess can itself become a decision risk.

Maintain, Adapt, Reverse

Decision Reassessment is not binary. The choice is not simply between holding or abandoning. There are three distinct paths.

Maintain
The original reasoning remains valid. Context has not changed materially. Execution continues.
Adapt
The underlying decision remains sound, but some assumptions or implementation conditions have shifted. The architecture or execution is modified.
Reverse
A critical assumption or condition has been invalidated. Continuing would require defending reasoning no longer supported by evidence.

The existence of these three paths reduces false binary thinking. Many decisions that appear to require a stark choice between "continue" or "abandon" can instead benefit from precise adaptation.

The cost of having been right yesterday

A leader may have been entirely correct when the original decision was made. This can make Reassessment psychologically and organisationally harder.

Because the leader can rightly defend:

"The evidence supported the decision."

That statement may be true. But it answers a historical question. It does not answer:

"Does the evidence still support it?"

This is one of the central distinctions of the entire subject. Being right at the moment of decision does not guarantee being right in maintaining it.

"Being right when the decision was made does not guarantee being right in continuing to hold it."

An executive scenario

An organisation decides to enter a new international market.

At the moment of decision: demand indicators are strong, regulation is favourable, capital requirements are acceptable, competitive intensity is manageable, local partnerships are available. The decision is well-reasoned.

Nine months later: regulation changes materially, the cost of capital rises, a major competitor enters the market, the sector economics shift.

The original decision does not become retroactively wrong. The original reasoning may have been excellent. But maintaining the strategy without Reassessment would require assuming that conditions which no longer exist continue to hold.

The question is not: "Should we exit because conditions have changed?"

Nor: "Should we continue because the original decision was sound?"

The question is:

"Given what we know today, would we make the same decision?"

That is Decision Reassessment.

Past investment is not evidence about the future

What we have already invested
≠
Evidence about what we should do next
Capital deployed. People hired. Time spent. The CEO has publicly endorsed the decision. The Board approved it. Teams have reorganised around it. These investments are real. But they do not provide evidence that the decision remains correct. Past commitment may influence the emotional and political cost of changing course. It should not be confused with evidence about future value.

Decision Reassessment in executive hiring

An organisation appoints a senior executive because the role requires: transformation, international expansion, decentralisation, operational discipline, or another specific mandate.

Twelve months later the organisational context shifts. Perhaps an acquisition changes the perimeter. Perhaps the CEO changes. Perhaps strategy pivots. Perhaps the company moves from expansion to restructuring.

The question should not automatically become: "Was this the wrong appointment?"

The better question is: "Is the decision context in which this executive was selected still the context in which the role operates today?"

A candidate may still be precisely the person the organisation originally needed. The organisation may simply no longer need the same role. This distinction protects organisations from incorrectly diagnosing a contextual shift as a candidate failure.

Five questions before reopening a decision

1 of 5
What has concretely changed?
Identify the material change rather than reacting to general discomfort. The change must be specific and locatable within the original logic.
2 of 5
Which original assumption does this affect?
Connect the new information to the reasoning that supported the decision. If it does not affect any critical assumption, its decision weight is limited.
3 of 5
Would we have decided differently had we known this then?
This test measures materiality. If the answer is no, the new information may be relevant but is not necessarily a Reassessment trigger.
4 of 5
Are we responding to evidence or to an outcome?
Do not allow a single result to rewrite the original decision process. The distinction between Decision Quality and Outcome Quality remains valid within Reassessment.
5 of 5
If we were making this decision for the first time today, what would we do?
Temporarily remove the weight of past commitment and examine the present decision. This is the final question of Reassessment.

AI will make Reassessment easier — and harder

AI can make it simpler to monitor signals, compare scenarios, preserve decision records, identify changes in assumptions, and surface new evidence. But more signals can also produce more reasons to reopen decisions.

The challenge will therefore not simply be detecting change. It will be distinguishing:

Material change
Modifies one or more critical assumptions of the decision. Requires Reassessment.
vs
Informational noise
Variation in signal flow. Does not modify the underlying logic. Requires interpretation, not Reassessment.

AI can increase the frequency of information. Judgment must determine its decision relevance. The connection with the territory of Decision Traceability and Outcome Bias remains central.

A decision should remain faithful to reality, not to itself

Strong leadership requires conviction. But conviction should attach to the objective and to the quality of the reasoning — not permanently to a single historical choice.

The purpose of a decision is not to survive. Its purpose is to remain the best available answer to a problem. When the problem changes, the evidence shifts, or a critical assumption becomes invalid — Reassessment is not inconsistency. It is decision discipline.

A strong organisation must therefore be capable of holding two ideas simultaneously:

"We made the best possible decision with what we knew."

And:

"Given what we know now, we may need to decide again."
