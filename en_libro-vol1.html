Preface

Why This Volume Exists

RATIO was born from a simple and uncomfortable observation: 46% of executive selections fail within 18 months. Not due to lack of competence among those making the selection. Not because of inadequate candidates. Due to a process problem — and often, a problem of questions.

This volume collects the first ten insights published in the RATIO knowledge base: structured analyses on decision risk in executive hiring, built without invented statistics, without reassuring simplifications, without the illusion that a perfect process exists.

What you will find in these pages is a narrative thread running through ten chapters: from the visible and invisible cost of hiring mistakes, to the nature of cognitive biases, to the assumptions no one formulates but everyone follows, all the way to the decision architecture that distinguishes organizations that manage risk from those that merely suffer it.

Each chapter can be read independently. Read in sequence, they build a coherent framework for thinking about high-impact decisions in a more precise, more defensible — and more honest — way.

Chapter 01 Decision Risk · May 15, 2026

The Hidden Cost of a Bad Hire

A hiring mistake at the C-suite level is not measured only by direct cost. It is measured by the silent damage it leaves over time.

"The cost appears after the decision."

The Problem Nobody Quantifies

When a hire proves to be wrong, the first reaction is to contain it: replace the profile, restart the selection process. But this reading radically underestimates the real damage. An executive-level hiring mistake is not a point-in-time event — it is a deterioration process that extends over time.

Industry research agrees: the cost of a wrong hire at C-suite level ranges between 3 and 15 times the annual compensation of the role. But the figure becomes even more significant when you factor in the components that never appear in budgets: management time distracted by damage control, harm to company culture, wrong strategic decisions made in the interim, and high-performers who quietly leave the organization.

Realistic Scenario

A growing company selects a CFO to support a new phase of international expansion. The profile looks solid on paper: relevant experience, positive references, convincing interviews. After nine months, clear signals of structural incompatibility emerge — not between technical skills and the role, but between the management model brought by the executive and the one required by the context. The decision to proceed with replacement comes after an additional three months of waiting. The direct replacement cost is recorded. Everything else — delayed strategic financial decisions, friction with the team, acquisition opportunities left unevaluated, key managers who in the meantime began conversations with competitors — does not appear in any report. Yet it is real, and it is permanent.

3–15× Annual salary: average cost of a wrong C-suite hire
18 mo. Average time before the mistake is recognized and addressed
46% Of new executives fail within the first 18 months (source: DDI)

The Three Categories of Invisible Cost

Direct operational costs — These are the most visible: the cost of a repeated selection process, any contractual penalties, the onboarding period of the replacement. They typically represent the smallest portion of the total damage, but they are the only ones that enter financial reports.

Deferred strategic costs — Every decision made by the wrong executive produces effects that persist well beyond their tenure. An inadequate CFO may have set up control processes that take years to dismantle.

Cultural and systemic costs — The hardest to measure, but often the most devastating. An inadequate leader generates organizational noise: reversible decisions made with delay, high-performing collaborators who lose confidence and seek alternatives, conflicts that consume managerial energy.

Decision Stress Test

If That Decision Were Before the Board Today

Imagine explaining to the board today why that person was hired.

What evidence would you present?
What risks had been identified?
What signals had been ignored?

If the answers exist only in the memory of those who attended the interviews, the problem is not the candidate. It is the process.

"The cost of a hiring mistake is not the price of a replacement. It is the price of everything that happened — and didn't happen — in the meantime."

The Biases That Increase the Risk of Error

Halo Effect
A positive perceived quality leads to overestimating the overall profile, obscuring concrete risk signals.
Similarity Bias
Candidates who share communication style or background with the evaluator are rated more positively.
Confirmation Bias
Once a positive impression is formed, one unconsciously seeks confirmation and minimizes contrary evidence.
Decision Urgency
Pressure to close quickly compresses due diligence in the final phases, precisely when risk is highest.
Bad hires rarely fail in a single day.
They fail slowly — and that is precisely why they cost so much.

Chapter 02 Assessment · April 28, 2026

Why the CV Is Not Enough to Evaluate a High-Impact Candidate

The résumé describes the past. It does not predict behavior under pressure, nor the decision risk a candidate carries with them.

"What matters is not on the page."

The CV as a Marketing Tool, Not an Evaluation Tool

The curriculum vitae is a document optimized to create a favorable impression. It is not — and never has been — an objective evaluation tool. Every professional who knows the rules of the game learns to select, emphasize and contextualize their experiences in relation to the role they aspire to.

The problem is that many organizations continue to use the CV as the primary filter in executive selection, delegating to a self-referential document a decision worth millions of euros of potential impact.

74% Of executive profiles contain significant inaccuracies in stated responsibilities
8 sec Average first-read time of a CV by a senior recruiter
12% Of genuinely predictive selection criteria can be verified from a CV

What the CV Cannot Measure

  • Decision-making capacity under pressure — How does the candidate react when data is incomplete, time is limited, and consequences are irreversible?
  • Management of organizational conflict — Can they navigate power dynamics without destroying them?
  • Adaptation to the specific context — Excellent performance at a startup may be a negative predictor in a mature corporate environment.
  • Managerial ego resilience — Can they distinguish between their personal interest and the organization's interest when the two diverge?

"The résumé tells you where they've been. It doesn't tell you who they are under pressure — which is the only thing that truly matters in a critical role."

Evaluation Stress Test

If We Removed the CV

If we removed this candidate's CV and kept only:

Observed behaviors
Decisions made
Reactions under pressure
Narrative consistency

Would we still hire them? If the answer is no, the CV is driving the decision too much.

RATIO does not evaluate résumés.
It evaluates the decision risk the résumé does not show.

Chapter 03 Decision Intelligence · April 10, 2026

How to Reduce Decision Risk in Strategic Hiring

Strategic hires cannot rely on intuition. Structured methodologies exist to reduce error to measurable data.

"The quality of a decision is in the process behind it."

The most capable leaders know how to make fast decisions under conditions of uncertainty. But this becomes a risk when applied indiscriminately to hiring decisions, where confirmation bias, the halo effect, and time pressure combine in a particularly insidious way.

82% Of CEOs have made at least one wrong strategic hire in the last 3 years
67% Of these attribute the error to an "instinctive" assessment not supported by data
4× Higher probability of success with a structured process (McKinsey)

The Five Principles of a Low-Risk Process

1. Define the risk before meeting candidates. Before opening the selection, the team should answer: "What behaviors, in this context, could lead to failure?"

2. Separate the evaluation of technical competencies from the decisional profile. Technical competencies are verified with objective methods. The decisional profile requires scenarios, cases, simulations.

3. Structure the scoring process before the evaluation. The weights of criteria must be decided before meeting candidates, not adapted retrospectively.

4. Insert an independent review point. An external review — even partial — of the process significantly increases the quality of the final decision.

5. Document the reasoning, not just the choice. Traceability of the decision-making process is an investment, not a cost.

"A robust selection process is not slower. It is more precise. And precision is the only form of respect for what is at stake."

The best decisions do not eliminate risk.
They make it visible before it becomes a problem.

Why the Best Hiring Decisions Don't Start with the Candidate

The most effective organizations don't try to eliminate risk. They seek to reduce avoidable errors.

"The decision begins before the candidate."

For years I have observed companies invest enormous amounts of time and energy in the search for the "perfect candidate." Yet many of the most costly decisions continued to turn into avoidable errors. Over time I understood that the problem rarely lies in the candidate. The real problem arises much earlier.

It arises from questions that often no one asks.

The Problem
Do we really know what problem this person will need to solve?
Success
Have we precisely defined what success in this role means?
Context
Is there real alignment between the organizational context and expectations?
Risk
What would the consequences of a wrong decision be?

The Right Question

Most processes begin with: "Who are we looking for?" — That is the wrong question.

The right question is: "What risk are we trying to manage?"

Decision Stress Test

Before the Next Selection Process

Do we know precisely what error we are trying to avoid? If the answer is not immediate and shared by everyone, the process already has a critical risk point.

The most important decisions deserve
something more than intuition.

Chapter 05 Decision Risk · June 23, 2026

The Most Costly Hiring Mistake Happens Before the Interview

Organizations often attribute hiring errors to the wrong candidate. In reality, many decisions fail long before the candidate even enters the room.

"The mistake precedes the interview."

The reality that emerges from the analysis of executive failures is uncomfortable: most high-impact hiring errors do not originate during the selection process. They originate before it. They stem from a poorly defined decision.

The Four Structural Absences

There are four absences that recur in processes that produce costly errors:

  • Absence of a precise definition of the problem the role must solve over the next 18–24 months.
  • Absence of measurable success indicators. What does success look like at 12 months? In precise, observable, comparable terms.
  • Absence of a map of critical decisions associated with the role. What high-impact decisions will this person need to make? In what context?
  • Absence of an explicit risk definition. Defining risk is not organizational pessimism. It is the rational basis for calibrating the intensity of the process.
72% Of executive failures is attributable to organizational fit, not technical competence
61 days Average C-suite process duration — role definition occupies less than 10%
4× Organizations that explicitly define risk criteria make errors 4× less frequently
Bad hires rarely stem from a failed interview.
They stem from a poorly defined decision.

Wrong Decisions Don't Come from Insufficient Information. They Come from Wrong Assumptions.

Every important decision rests on dozens of invisible assumptions. The more wrong they are, the greater the risk.

"Assumptions are the silent architecture of every decision."

The reality that emerges from the analysis of decision failures tells a different story from what we expect. Wrong decisions rarely stem from a lack of information. They stem from wrong assumptions about that information. From unverified beliefs that the brain treats as facts.

We don't make decisions based on facts. We make decisions based on our interpretations of facts — which in turn rest on assumptions we have never explicitly formulated.

The Five Most Dangerous Assumptions

Experience = Competence
Having held a role for many years is interpreted as mastery. In reality, experience measures exposure, not the quality of learning.
Confidence = Leadership
Candidates who show no doubt are perceived as more capable. Research suggests the opposite: the ability to sustain uncertainty is a more reliable indicator.
Cultural Fit = Adaptability
Cultural affinity is confused with adaptive capacity — which is the opposite. Familiarity reduces initial friction but does not predict performance under stress.
Past Success = Future Success
Track record is treated as a predictor without analyzing the context in which it was built. An excellent CFO in stable markets may lack the tools for volatile contexts.
68% Of decision makers cannot list the 3 main assumptions behind their last strategic decision
3.2× More likely to have a negative outcome when core assumptions are not made explicit
11 min Average time spent verifying assumptions in a 90-minute decision-making session
Information reduces uncertainty.
Wrong assumptions recreate it — invisibly.

Chapter 07 Executive Hiring · July 6, 2026

The Most Dangerous Assumption in Executive Hiring

Every executive hiring decision begins with assumptions. Organizations that recognize them make better decisions. Those that don't often pay for invisible mistakes.

"The decision begins long before the candidate enters the room."

The decision does not begin in the interview room. It begins weeks earlier, in a silent moment when someone reads a name for the first time, notices a professional title, or receives a recommendation from a trusted colleague. In that instant, a hypothesis is formed. And from that moment on, everything that follows is shaped — often invisibly — by that first assumption.

The hiring decision is not made during the interview. It is made the first time someone forms an opinion about the candidate — and that opinion is built entirely on assumptions.

The Five Most Dangerous Assumptions in Executive Hiring

Previous Scale = Future Capacity
Having led a large organization is interpreted as a reliable predictor for different contexts. In reality, scale measures exposure, not transferability.
Employer Brand = Candidate Quality
Coming from a prestigious company automatically transfers credibility. This assumption ignores the individual contribution from within a successful system.
Confidence = Decision Depth
Confidence can be communication style, not analytical depth. The ability to sustain uncertainty is a more reliable indicator of decision quality.
The Process Is Objective
The organization assumes its own process generates objective data. In reality every phase is built on implicit assumptions that determine what information is collected and how.
72% Of negative decisions was preceded by a process described as "solid" by participants
2.8× More likely to have a negative outcome when assumptions are not made explicit
9 min Average time spent explicitly verifying assumptions across 6 weeks of selection
Organizations don't make mistakes because they make decisions.
They make mistakes because they rarely interrogate the assumptions those decisions are built on.

The Silent Cost of Consensus

The most dangerous executive decisions rarely begin with missing data. They begin when consensus arrives too early and is mistaken for judgment.

"Consensus is not judgment. It is the absence of it."

In more structured organizations, consensus is often treated as an indicator of decision quality. If everyone agrees, the decision must be good. If no one objects, the risk has been correctly evaluated.

This belief is one of the most dangerous in executive decision making. Easy consensus is not a signal of clarity. It is often a signal that something important has not been said.

When everyone immediately agrees, the question to ask is not "did we make the right decision?" — it is "who decided not to dissent, and why?"

Three Mechanisms That Produce Premature Consensus

Hierarchy silences dissent. In organizations where ranking is visible, the opinion of the decision maker with the highest rank tends to assume normative status before it is even discussed.

Time pressure compresses debate. When decisions must be made quickly, the cognitive system favors shortcuts — including rapid alignment around the option that already seems acceptable to the majority.

Group cohesion produces illusions of invulnerability. Highly cohesive teams tend to develop excessive confidence in their collective judgment, reducing the threshold of attention to risk signals.

Decision Stress Test

Before Closing a Decision with Unanimous Consensus

Who in this room had an objection they did not formulate?
Did consensus emerge from a shared analysis or from implicit pressure not to dissent?
Did we actively seek contrary perspectives before converging?

Consensus that has not been earned is the risk that has not been identified.

Dissent is not a problem to be managed.
It is information to be processed.

The Greatest Risk Is Deciding Well on the Wrong Problem

A decision can be perfectly rational and still be wrong. The risk arises when an organization solves the wrong problem with precision.

"The right answer to the wrong question is still wrong."

There is a category of decision error that is particularly insidious because it produces no clear dysfunction signals in the short term. The organization correctly analyzes available data. It selects the right tool. It executes with precision. And it still produces the wrong result — because the problem it solved was not the real problem.

The risk is not making the wrong decision. The risk is making the right decision for the wrong problem.

The Problem Frame Test

Before building a solution, five questions that can reveal whether the problem has been correctly defined:

The Problem or the Symptom?
What we are trying to solve — is it the real problem, or its surface manifestation?
Who Defined It?
Does the problem framing reflect the perspective of those who experience it or those who have the power to name it?
What Assumption Supports It?
What is the implicit belief that makes this the problem to solve? Has that belief been verified?
What If We Got It Wrong?
If the problem were defined differently, would the solution we are building still be relevant?
The quality of a decision does not depend on the quality of the answer.
It depends on the quality of the question that generated it.

Chapter 10 Executive Hiring · August 11, 2026 · Cristiano Ludovisi

Why Executive Hiring Decisions Fail Before the Interview Even Begins

The most important error in an executive selection can occur before the first candidate even enters the room. The risk originates from the quality of the decision built around the role.

"The interview is too late. The decision starts earlier."

There is a moment in the executive hiring process when risk has already been built into the decision — silently, structurally, before speaking with any candidate. It is the moment the role is defined.

Not the job description — which is an operational document. The definition of the role as a response to a specific organizational problem, in a specific context, with specific expectations. This work rarely receives the attention it deserves. And its absence is the primary cause of failure in executive selections.

The Process Before the Process

PHASE 0 — Before Everything
Definition of the decisional context
What specific problem must this role solve? What are the first three critical decisions this person will need to make?
PHASE 1
Definition of success indicators
How will we measure success at 12 months? With what precise and observable indicators — not generic ones.
PHASE 2
Mapping of critical assumptions
What implicit beliefs are guiding the construction of the profile? Have they been verified or taken for granted?
PHASE 3
Only now: the selection process begins
With explicit, documented and shared criteria. The interview becomes a verification of precise hypotheses, not a collection of general impressions.
Decision Stress Test

Before Opening Any Executive Selection

What specific problem must this role solve in the next 18 months?
What will be the first three critical decisions this person will need to make?
How will we measure success at 12 months? With what precise indicators?
What is the organizational cost of an error in this role?

If the answers are not documented before the selection begins, the interview cannot compensate for this absence.

The quality of an executive selection
is not measured by the candidate chosen.
It is measured by the quality of the questions
that preceded that choice.
