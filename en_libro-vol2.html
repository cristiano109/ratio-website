Preface

From assumption risk to decision risk

RATIO's Volume I examined decision risk in executive hiring: the hidden cost of mistakes, the cognitive biases, the invisible assumptions that guide choices about candidates.

This second volume widens the lens. The ten insights collected in these pages no longer concern only the selection of people, but the architecture of executive judgment itself: how data can create false confidence, how experience can turn into vulnerability, how a good outcome can conceal a weak decision — and how a bad outcome can coexist with impeccable judgment.

The narrative thread running through these ten chapters is the recurring, uncomfortable distinction between what happens and what we decided: between outcome and decision quality, between consensus and authority, between having been approved and being truly ready to act. It is a distinction organisations tend to compress — and one RATIO tries, insight after insight, to make visible again.

As in the first volume, every chapter can be read on its own. Read in sequence, they build an infrastructure of thought for governing high-impact decisions with more rigour — and fewer illusions.

Chapter 11 Decision Intelligence · August 18, 2026

Why more data doesn't necessarily lead to better executive decisions

The challenge facing modern organisations is no longer access to information. It is understanding which information deserves to influence a decision.

"Between data and decision lies something no dashboard can remove: judgment."

For decades, the limiting factor of corporate decision-making was scarcity of information. Today the problem has reversed: real-time dashboards, business intelligence, predictive analytics and AI systems give executives more data than an entire organisation could process just a few decades ago. Yet one question remains open: is more information actually producing better decisions? Not necessarily.

Data, evidence, judgment, decision

A data point is information. It becomes evidence only when it is relevant to the decision being made. An organisation can own hundreds of metrics on a problem and still have very little truly useful evidence. Every step from data to decision requires a transformation: selection, interpretation, contextualisation, comparison. And it is precisely in these steps that a significant part of decision risk is born.

More Data ≠ Better Evidence
Better Evidence ≠ Better Judgment
Better Judgment ≠ Better Decision

The abundance paradox and confirmation bias

When information is scarce, the problem is obvious: we don't know enough. When it becomes abundant, the problem changes nature: we no longer know what deserves attention. An executive can walk into a meeting with fifty slides of analysis and leave more confident — but greater confidence doesn't mean greater decision quality. Worse, when enough information exists, it becomes possible to find evidence compatible with almost any hypothesis: those who believe a market is promising will find growth data; those who are against it will find risk data. More data can therefore make confirmation bias more sophisticated, not eliminate it.

The precision of an analysis can be mistaken for the quality of a decision. But a model can be extremely precise in answering the wrong question.

The question first, the analysis second

In executive hiring this problem is particularly visible: more interviews, more assessments, more references, more scores can simply increase the confidence with which the organisation makes a decision built on a wrong assumption. If the role was defined poorly, more data on the candidate does not fix the problem. Before increasing the quantity of information, you must increase the quality of the problem definition.

Artificial intelligence will multiply the availability of data.
It will not automatically multiply the quality of the judgment that interprets it.

Chapter 12 Leadership Judgment · August 26, 2026

When experience becomes a decision risk

Why the most experienced leaders can be the most vulnerable when context changes. How to separate accumulated experience from its actual contextual relevance.

"Experience recognises the past. Judgment must decide whether it is still relevant."

Experience is one of the most valuable assets in leadership: it allows pattern recognition, reduces the time needed to understand a situation, helps distinguish what matters from what can be ignored. But this very capability contains a risk. Experience can accelerate a decision before we have verified whether the problem we face is really the same one we already know.

The expertise paradox

The more experience we accumulate, the larger our library of patterns becomes — and the more analogies we can draw on to interpret a new situation. The problem is not having experience. It arises when experience stops being evidence and becomes a premise.

"It worked then, so it will probably work again." This conclusion can be correct. Or it can be one of the most costly assumptions inside a decision.

What can have changed between "then" and "now"

The market
Competitive conditions, demand, pricing structure.
Technology
New solutions can invalidate established advantages.
Organisational culture
People, values and competitive speed evolve.
The power structure
Available capital, customer expectations, level of uncertainty.

The decision may seem familiar. The context might not be at all.

Three levels not to be confused

An important decision should distinguish at least three levels: What We Know (verified facts, observable data), What We Infer (conclusions drawn from interpretation) and What We Assume (unverified premises that silently influence judgment). Experience compresses these three levels into a synthetic judgment — "it doesn't convince me", "it's the right profile" — which for this very reason is rarely challenged, even though it may hide twenty years of assumptions never updated.

Decision Stress Test

Before relying on an "expert" judgment

Is this situation really similar to the one I think I remember?
What, among market, technology, people and culture, has changed since then?
Am I compressing complexity or genuinely understanding it?

If the judgment is fast but answers to these questions don't exist, speed is probably replacing rigour.

Executive hiring is the place where the past weighs most.
"Succeeded there" does not equal "will succeed here".

Chapter 13 Decision Intelligence · September 2, 2026

The problem isn't making a bad decision. It's not knowing why we made it.

Organisations measure the results of decisions. Far more rarely do they preserve the logic that existed at the moment those decisions were made.

"A past decision should not be judged with information that belongs to the future."

When an outcome is negative, it is easy to conclude the decision was wrong. But that judgment often uses information that only became available afterwards. The outcome rewrites the past — and outcome quality and decision quality are not the same thing.

The most dangerous quadrant is not failure

The relationship between decision quality and outcome quality produces four scenarios: a good decision with a good outcome (Deserved Success, worth replicating); a good decision with a bad outcome (Unlucky Decision, not to be punished); a visible failure born of a weak decision (Deserved/Visible Failure, which at least teaches something); and the most insidious quadrant of all — a weak decision that still produces a good result.

Dangerous Luck — the most dangerous quadrant

Fragile assumptions, insufficient evidence, alternatives never truly considered — yet the outcome is positive. The organisation concludes: "We decided well." The weak process gets replicated, reinforced, turned into method. Until the moment luck stops compensating for its fragility.

Decision Traceability

Organisations are equipped to preserve outcomes: dashboards, KPIs, revenue, EBITDA. Far less often do they preserve the decision itself: what problem was being solved, which assumptions were considered true, which alternatives were discarded and why, which risk was deemed acceptable. Without this trace, the outcome is recorded and the decision evaporates.

"Organisations build a memory of outcomes. Decision Intelligence also requires a memory of decisions."

The Decision Learning Loop

01 → 02
Decision → Expected Outcome
What we decide and what we expect it to produce.
03 → 04
Actual Outcome → Gap Analysis
What actually happened and where the gap lies.
05 → 06
Learning → Better Next Decision
The cycle continues, decision after decision.
Without a trace, true decision learning does not exist.
"Bad hire" is an explanation. It is not yet a diagnosis.

Chapter 14
Chapter 14 Decision Intelligence · September 9, 2026

A good decision can produce a bad outcome. And vice versa.

Organisations often evaluate the quality of a decision by looking at the outcome it produced. This is dangerous. Decision quality and outcome quality are not the same thing.

The decision answers one question: was it justified to decide this way, given what we knew? The outcome answers a different question: what actually happened in the real world? These two questions must remain separate before being related to each other.

Outcome Bias

We judge the quality of a past decision almost automatically using knowledge of its outcome — not an analysis of the process that preceded it. When we know something went well, the process seems more solid than it was. When we know it went badly, it seems weaker. Outcome Bias rewards weak judgment with lucky outcomes and penalises solid judgment with unlucky outcomes — building executive reputations based on outcomes rather than decision competence.

Deserved SuccessGood decision, good outcome — replicate the method
Unlucky DecisionGood decision, bad outcome — don't change the process
Dangerous LuckBad decision, good outcome — the most dangerous quadrant

"Conviction has value. But a positive result does not prove the process that generated it was sound."

Organisations that punish correct judgment

Organisations that systematically penalise good decisions with bad outcomes end up developing risk aversion where methodological rigour should exist instead. Their leaders learn to protect their own reputation, not to improve the quality of their reasoning. That is why distinguishing the four quadrants is not an academic exercise: it is a condition for learning from decisions rather than from luck.

A good decision increases the probability of a desirable outcome.
It cannot guarantee it. And that does not make it any less good.

Chapter 15 Decision Intelligence · September 16, 2026

Changing a decision doesn't necessarily mean you were wrong.

A decision can be correct when it is made and become wrong to maintain once the conditions that made it rational change.

No consequential decision is universally correct: it is correct in relation to the information available, the reasonable assumptions, the objectives and constraints of that moment. A decision can be sound at T1 and become inappropriate at T2 — not because the organisation got it wrong at T1, but because reality changed.

"A decision should not be protected by information that did not exist when it was made."

Assumption Half-Life

Every strategic decision contains assumptions: about market demand, about capital availability, about a competitor's behaviour. Some assumptions remain valid for years. Others become obsolete in weeks. Organisations should ask not only "what assumptions are we making?" but also "for how long can we reasonably expect them to remain valid?" The flow is simple: Assumption → Validity → New Signals → Reassessment.

Consistency is not decision quality

Senior leaders are often expected to demonstrate conviction. Reversing a communicated direction can seem costly: a CEO fears appearing uncertain, a Board resists reopening an already approved decision. But consistency only has value as long as the underlying reasoning remains valid.

"Conviction has value. Attachment to invalidated assumptions does not."

Not every signal justifies a new decision

This principle is not an invitation to continuously revisit every choice. A filter is needed: a Decision Reassessment Gate that distinguishes everyday noise from signals that truly undermine the critical assumptions on which the decision was based.

Changing course is not always a repudiation of the past.
It can be proof that judgment is still active.

Chapter 16 Decision Intelligence · September 23, 2026

Not deciding is already a decision. And it has a cost.

Organisations carefully measure the risk of acting. Far less often do they measure the risk that accumulates while they wait to decide.

"Decision risk does not exist only in action. It can exist in delay. It can exist in inaction."

Delay is not neutral: Option Loss

At T1 an organisation may have five options available. At T2, after waiting, two may have disappeared. The organisation used the time to gather better information — but it also lost optionality. This phenomenon, which we call Option Loss, is one of the main components of Decision Latency and rarely appears in an analysis that justifies waiting.

The three dimensions of decision risk

01 — Risk of Action
What can happen if we decide and act? The most visible risk, the one in business cases.
02 — Risk of Delay
What can change while we wait? Market, costs, people, time windows.
03 — Risk of Inaction
What happens if no significant decision is made? The status quo still produces effects.

The waiting equation

Every wait contains a trade-off: on one side the value of additional information, on the other the cost of delay (loss of options, context change, opportunity cost, execution delay, organisational drift). Waiting is rational only as long as the expected value of the new information remains higher than the cost the wait generates.

"Some uncertainty can be reduced. Some uncertainty can only be decided through."

The illusion of certainty

Behind many prolonged waits lies an implicit expectation: that by continuing to analyse, uncertainty will eventually disappear. Often it does not. At that point additional analysis stops reducing uncertainty and starts merely postponing exposure to it — right at the point where executive judgment becomes indispensable.

Waiting is not free.
It simply has a cost that never appears in the income statement.

Chapter 17 Decision Intelligence · September 30, 2026

When everyone participates in the decision, but no one is accountable for it.

Consensus can be broad, information complete and the process can look rigorous. But if it isn't clear who has the authority to decide, accountability risks dissolving exactly when it becomes necessary.

An organisation can involve many people, gather solid evidence and reach apparent agreement without ever having clarified who is actually authorised to decide. The question is not how many people participated, but whether, at the end of the process, it is clear who decides, on what criteria and with what accountability.

Four roles that must not be confused

01 — Decision Input
Who provides evidence and perspectives. Informational contribution does not automatically confer authority.
02 — Decision Recommendation
Who compares alternatives and formulates a reasoned proposal.
03 — Decision Authority
Who holds the formal mandate to choose among alternatives.
04 — Decision Accountability
Who answers for assigned responsibilities and subsequent review.

Consensus is not a substitute for authority

When consensus becomes an implicit, unlimited condition, every stakeholder acquires a power of deferral that doesn't match their formal responsibility. The decision gets discussed, revised, postponed — no one explicitly blocks it, but no one manages to conclude it either.

CONSULTATION ≠ APPROVAL · CONSENSUS ≠ AUTHORITY · PARTICIPATION ≠ OWNERSHIP

The cost of ambiguous authority

A proposal moves from one function to another. Every handoff produces new comments but no new evidence. The result is a precise chain: unclear authority → repeated consultation → diffused responsibility → decision latency → option loss. The organisation may interpret this movement as prudence, when part of the delay actually stems from a governance problem.

RATIO Principle #17

Shared Intelligence. Clear Authority. Visible Accountability.

An effective owner doesn't eliminate dissent: they organise its contribution. They don't replace evidence with their own authority: they verify that the relevant evidence has been considered.

Chapter 18
Chapter 18 Decision Intelligence · October 7, 2026

Dissent doesn't slow a good decision. It tests it.

A decision can have solid data, a clear owner and a rigorous process. But if no one can genuinely challenge the dominant hypothesis, judgment remains vulnerable.

In the previous chapter we distinguished Decision Input, Recommendation, Authority and Accountability. This architecture is necessary but not sufficient: a CEO can have a crystal-clear mandate and a Board can follow every procedure, yet the dominant hypothesis can travel through the entire process without ever meeting genuine intellectual opposition.

Clear authority tells us who decides. Constructive dissent helps us test what is being decided.

The risk of premature convergence

Executive decisions often start with multiple alternatives, then the process converges on a preferred direction. The risk arises when convergence happens before the decisive assumptions have been sufficiently tested: the decision becomes progressively more coherent without necessarily becoming more robust.

The RATIO Decision Challenge

Before commitment, a consequential decision can be tested across five dimensions: Problem (are we solving the right problem?), Assumptions (which ones must be true for it to work?), Evidence (what evidence supports it, and what could contradict it?), Alternatives (which alternative did we discard too quickly?), Failure Conditions (what would have to happen for it to prove inadequate?).

Questions that separate the challenge from the person
Not just: "Does everyone agree?"
But: "What would have to be true for us to be wrong?"
What is the strongest argument against the choice we're about to make?

Right to Challenge ≠ Right to Veto. Dissent protects the reasoning; authority protects the conclusion.

Challenge the Reasoning. Protect the Authority.
Strengthen the Decision.

Chapter 19 Decision Intelligence · October 14, 2026

A decision is not complete when it is made. It becomes complete when it can be executed.

A decision can be rational, well documented and formally approved. But without the conditions to turn it into action, the risk isn't eliminated: it changes shape.

"A decision can be approved without yet being ready for execution."

A Board approves a strategy. A CEO authorises an investment. The decision has been made — but resources may not be available and some essential conditions may not be met. Organisations devote great attention to choosing the best alternative. Far less often do they check, with the same rigour, whether they have the conditions to implement it.

Three conditions not to be confused

Decision CommitmentWhat have we decided?
Execution ReadinessAre we in a position to act?
Execution ResultsWhat is our action actually producing?

DECISION COMMITMENT ≠ EXECUTION READINESS ≠ EXECUTION SUCCESS.

The gap between decision and action

This is the distance between a formally made decision and the organisation's actual ability to begin implementing it — not just a matter of time, but of competencies, resources, coordination and still-missing responsibilities. While this distance is being closed, the context keeps changing.

The RATIO Ownership Gate: seven conditions

Before launching a high-impact decision, an organisation can verify seven conditions: Decision, Owner, Authority, Input, Rights, Escalation, Accountability. It is not an automatic system, but a check of organisational clarity — and a direct callback to a principle already introduced: Decision Accountability is not an Outcome Guarantee.

RATIO Principle #19

Role First. Candidate Second.

An excellent appointment does not automatically create an organisation ready to support it.

Chapter 20 Decision Intelligence · October 21, 2026

A decision doesn't stop being risky once we start executing it.

Every strategic decision is built on assumptions about context, resources and the future. But those assumptions can change while the organisation acts.

"A decision can remain formally in force even when the assumptions supporting it are no longer valid."

A Board approves a strategy, responsibilities are clear, resources considered. Execution begins — but context does not stand still: demand can change, costs can rise, a critical competency can become unavailable. The decision remains formally in force. Some of the assumptions that justified its approval may, however, no longer be valid.

Two distinct dimensions

In the previous chapter we introduced execution readiness. But even once those conditions are met, another question remains: for how long will the initial assumptions continue to be valid? Execution readiness and assumption validity are two different dimensions of decision risk.

Not all assumptions carry the same weight

Secondary Assumptions
Their change requires limited adjustments.
Relevant Assumptions
They influence costs, timelines, capacity or expected results.
Critical Assumptions
The very sustainability of the decision depends on them.
Assumption Drift
The gradual, often silent, departure from initial hypotheses.

The RATIO Decision Assumption Monitor

A five-element model links critical assumptions to the signals that indicate their change: Critical Assumption → Original Evidence → Signal to Observe → Attention Threshold → Decision Response. Monitoring results is necessary, but not always sufficient: good current performance ≠ guaranteed validity of future assumptions.

"The most dangerous change is not always the sudden one. It can be the one the organisation has learned to consider normal."

RATIO Principle #20

Role First. Candidate Second.

The quality of an executive decision also depends on knowing when the context is no longer the same.

Decision risk doesn't end with approval.
It keeps living on — silently — through execution.
