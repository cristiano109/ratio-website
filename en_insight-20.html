Every strategic decision rests on assumptions about context, resources and the future. But those assumptions can change while the organisation acts. Risk does not lie only in choosing on the basis of wrong assumptions. It also lies in continuing to execute a decision without verifying whether the assumptions that supported it are still valid.

A decision can remain formally valid while its logic changes

A board of directors approves a strategy. A chief executive authorises an investment. An organisation launches a transformation.

The decision has been analysed, discussed and approved. Responsibilities are clear. Resources have been considered. The initial conditions allow the organisation to proceed.

Execution begins. But the context does not remain still.

Demand may change. Costs may rise. A technology may alter competitive conditions. A fundamental capability may become unavailable.

The decision remains formally in force. But some of the assumptions that justified its approval may no longer be valid.

This is where an often invisible decision risk emerges. Organisations monitor the results of their activities. Far less frequently do they verify whether the conditions on which they founded their choices continue to exist.

"A decision can remain formally in force
even when the assumptions that support it are no longer valid."

A decision does not live only in the moment it is made. It continues to be exposed to reality throughout its execution.

Risk does not end when the organisation begins to act

In Insight #19 we introduced execution readiness. An approved decision is not necessarily an executable decision. The organisation must understand the resources, responsibilities, dependencies and conditions required to act.

But even when these conditions are met, another question remains. For how long will the initial assumptions continue to be valid?

Execution readiness verifies the conditions to begin. Assumption monitoring verifies whether some fundamental conditions continue to support the decision over time. These are two distinct dimensions.

RATIO — Two Distinct Dimensions
01
Execution Readiness
Do we possess the conditions to begin?
02
Assumption Validity
Are the conditions that justify our choice still sufficiently valid?

"Being ready to begin does not mean
that the initial conditions will remain unchanged."

Decision quality therefore requires a distinction between the soundness of the initial choice and the persistence of the conditions that support it.

Assumptions are part of the structure of a decision

Every important decision contains assumptions. Some are stated explicitly. Others remain implicit.

An investment may presuppose that demand will grow. A transformation may presuppose that certain capabilities will be available. An international expansion may depend on the stability of certain regulatory conditions. An executive appointment may presuppose that the mandate and the organisational context remain consistent with those originally defined.

Not all these assumptions are wrong. And it is not possible to eliminate every assumption from a decision. Executive judgement also consists of deciding in the presence of incomplete information. But a distinction must be made between what we know and what we consider sufficiently probable.

RATIO — Three Levels of Reasoning
01
Evidence
What is supported by the information available.
02
Assumption
What we consider plausible or necessary to support the decision, but which is not fully verified.
03
Uncertainty
What could alter the context, the validity of the assumptions, or the consequences of the choice.
EVIDENCE ≠ ASSUMPTION
ASSUMPTION ≠ CERTAINTY

A robust decision is not free of assumptions. It is a decision in which the relevant assumptions are recognisable and can be tested.

Not all assumptions carry the same weight

A strategic decision can contain dozens of hypotheses. Monitoring all of them with the same intensity would be inefficient.

Some concern secondary details. Others affect the speed of execution. Others still can change the value of the entire choice. A distinction must therefore be made.

RATIO — Levels of Criticality
01
Secondary Assumptions
Hypotheses whose change may require limited adjustments, without materially altering the logic of the decision.
02
Relevant Assumptions
Hypotheses that influence costs, timing, capacity or expected results and may require significant changes to the path.
03
Critical Assumptions
Hypotheses on which the sustainability or the fundamental justification of the decision depends.

"Not every assumption deserves the same monitoring.
Critical assumptions deserve visibility proportionate to the risk they carry."

The point is not to indefinitely increase the number of controls. It is to identify which changes could truly alter the original judgement.

We introduce a new conceptual model, whose purpose is to connect critical assumptions to the signals that may indicate a change. The model distinguishes five elements.

The silent change of assumptions

Not all conditions change suddenly. Some change progressively.

A cost rises slowly. A capability becomes increasingly difficult to source. A competitive advantage erodes. A commercial relationship gradually loses importance. Market behaviour evolves.

Each change, observed in isolation, may seem manageable. But their accumulation can alter the original logic of the decision.

We define this phenomenon decision assumption drift: the progressive divergence of observed conditions from the hypotheses on which the choice was founded.

Drift does not automatically mean that the decision has become wrong. It means that its logic may require a new review.

"The most dangerous change is not always the sudden one.
It can be the one the organisation has learned to consider normal."

Monitoring results is not the same as monitoring assumptions

Organisations have performance indicators. Revenue. Margins. Productivity. Market share. Delivery times.

These indicators are important. But they mostly describe what is happening. They do not always explain whether the original logic of the decision is still sustainable.

Consider an investment based on the assumption that a given technology will remain competitive for five years. In the first twelve months the economic results might be positive. But in the meantime an alternative technology might emerge capable of radically changing the market.

Current results are not necessarily negative. The original assumption, however, may have become more fragile.

RATIO — Two Types of Observation
01
Outcome Indicators
What is our decision actually producing?
02
Validity Indicators
Are the conditions on which we decided still sufficiently valid?
GOOD CURRENT PERFORMANCE ≠ GUARANTEED FUTURE VALIDITY OF ASSUMPTIONS

Monitoring results is necessary. But it is not always sufficient to recognise a change in decision risk in time.

Early signals and late signals

An organisation can recognise a problem when the consequences are already evident. An investment loses value. A transformation accumulates delays. A market generates revenue below expectations.

At that point the need to intervene may be clear. But some signals could have emerged earlier.

RATIO — Two Categories of Signals
01
Early Signals
Information that may indicate a change in relevant conditions before significant effects appear in the results.
02
Late Signals
Information that confirms consequences already produced or well under way.

An early signal is not a certain prediction. It can be ambiguous, temporary or misleading. For this reason it must be interpreted.

"A signal is not a conclusion.
It is a reason to verify whether the original reasoning remains valid."

Decision quality does not depend only on the ability to observe signals. It depends on the ability to establish which signals genuinely deserve attention.

An executive scenario: the industrial transformation

A manufacturing group approves an investment to automate a significant part of production. The project rests on three fundamental assumptions. Demand will remain sufficiently stable. Energy costs will be compatible with forecasts. The selected technology will maintain an operational advantage over available alternatives.

The board approves the investment. The organisation verifies the conditions for execution and launches the project.

After a few months new signals emerge. A major customer revises its purchasing forecasts. Energy costs rise. A competitor introduces an alternative technology with potentially relevant characteristics.

None of these events automatically proves that the investment must be abandoned. But all of them can affect the original assumptions.

Monitoring oriented exclusively towards project progress might limit itself to verifying compliance with budget and schedule. Assumption monitoring instead asks a different question:

"If we had to decide today, with this new information,
would the logic of the investment still be sufficiently sound?"

The answer might confirm the project. It might require an adjustment. It might suggest modifying certain phases. Or it might justify a broader reassessment by the authorised parties.

The presence of new signals does not automatically make the initial decision wrong. But ignoring them can make continuing it progressively less conscious.

When should a signal become a reassessment?

There is a fundamental distinction. Observing a change does not mean the decision must be immediately modified. If every variation triggered a strategic review, the organisation would risk paralysis. Three conditions must be distinguished.

RATIO — From Observation to Reassessment
01
Ordinary Monitoring
The information observed remains compatible with the critical assumptions and with the margins of uncertainty considered. The decision proceeds.
02
Heightened Attention
A signal calls a relevant condition into question, but the information is not yet sufficient to conclude that the logic of the choice has materially changed. A targeted review is needed.
03
Decision Reassessment
New evidence indicates that a critical assumption may no longer be sustainable, or that residual risk has undergone a material change. The matter must be submitted to the appropriate decision authority.
OBSERVING ≠ REASSESSING
REASSESSING ≠ REVOKING
REASSESSING ≠ ADMITTING A MISTAKE

Monitoring recognises signals. Reassessment examines their meaning. The competent authority decides whether the path needs to change.

Thresholds must not create a false sense of precision

Thresholds can be used to identify relevant variations. A cost increase. A decline in demand. A change in expected timing. The loss of a critical capability.

But not every decision can be described by a number. Some assumptions depend on qualitative factors. Trust. Leadership. Reputation. Organisational behaviours. Stability of relationships.

For this reason a decision threshold must not be interpreted as an automatic mechanism. It can be quantitative, qualitative or based on specific events. Its value depends on its ability to make a change that deserves judgement recognisable.

"A threshold does not replace judgement.
It indicates when judgement must be exercised again."

The apparent precision of an indicator does not guarantee the quality of its interpretation.

Who should observe the assumptions?

Insight #17 introduced decision accountability. Assumption monitoring adds an organisational question. Who holds the responsibility for recognising and communicating a relevant change?

The authority that approved a decision may not be the party that observes operational conditions on a daily basis. A commercial manager may recognise a change in demand. The finance director may detect a deterioration in economic conditions. The operations director may identify the loss of a critical capability.

This information must be able to reach whoever holds the authority to assess potential changes to the choice. The responsibility to observe does not automatically equate to the authority to change course.

OBSERVATION RESPONSIBILITY ≠ DECISION AUTHORITY

The value of monitoring also depends on the organisation's ability to connect relevant information to the parties who can act on it.

Artificial intelligence can observe more signals. Not automatically decide what they mean.

Artificial intelligence can help organisations identify variations, compare scenarios and analyse large amounts of information. It can flag anomalies. It can compare current data against prior hypotheses. It can highlight trends that are difficult to recognise manually.

But a signal detected by a system does not automatically amount to a new decision truth. Correlations can be unstable. Data can be incomplete. A change can be temporary. A model can interpret as significant something that does not actually change the logic of the decision.

MORE SIGNALS DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY MEAN BETTER JUDGEMENT
DETECTING A CHANGE DOES NOT MEAN UNDERSTANDING ITS CONSEQUENCES

Artificial intelligence can help make information more visible. But its relevance to a decision requires interpretation, context and accountability.

The problem is not only seeing earlier. It is understanding what deserves a decision response.

Executive hiring: the assumptions about the role can change too

An organisation appoints a new chief executive. The decision is built around a role, a context and certain objectives. The candidate has competencies consistent with the expected conditions. The appointment is approved.

But in the following months the organisation faces a significant change. A new acquisition alters the company's scope. The market demands different priorities. The responsibilities of the role expand. Some expected resources become unavailable.

The question should not only be: "Is the executive achieving the expected results?" It should include: "Is the role and the context against which we evaluated this person still the same?"

A material change in conditions can alter the nature of the responsibilities required. This does not automatically prove that the original appointment was wrong. It means that the assumptions about the role must be re-examined.

This is the continuity of the RATIO principle:

RATIO Principle
Role First.
Then the Candidate.
The quality of an executive decision also depends on recognising when the context is no longer the same.

This verification is not an automatic system of approval or revocation. It does not guarantee the prediction of events. It makes more recognisable the moment at which the logic of a decision deserves to be re-examined.

Monitoring does not mean continuously questioning everything

An organisation cannot reassess every decision at every change in context. It would be inefficient. And it could prevent execution.

Assumption monitoring must therefore be selective and proportionate. It does not require a continuous search for reasons to doubt. It does not introduce a generalised right to block decisions. It does not replace operational accountability.

It serves to distinguish between normal fluctuations of reality and changes capable of materially altering the reasons that support a choice.

STABILITY DOES NOT MEAN IGNORING CHANGE
ADAPTABILITY DOES NOT MEAN CHANGING DIRECTION AT EVERY SIGNAL

A good decision must be able to be executed with discipline. But discipline must not turn into an inability to recognise when reality has altered the initial conditions.

RATIO operates in the territory of executive decision intelligence. Its objective is to make more visible the quality of reasoning, assumptions, evidence, context, uncertainty and risk associated with significant decisions.

With decision assumption monitoring we introduce a new conceptual dimension. It is not enough to understand why a decision was made. It is useful to understand what conditions supported its validity, and what changes might require a new evaluation.

This does not turn RATIO into a service of continuous operational surveillance. It does not mean that RATIO automatically monitors company activities. It does not replace management systems, executives or corporate bodies. Its contribution remains in the quality of the decision-making process: making more visible the premises that matter and the conditions under which judgement might need to be exercised again.

"RATIO supports judgement.
It does not replace the authority of those who decide."

The final principle

A decision can be well built. It can rest on solid evidence. It can have explicit assumptions. It can have undergone a critical review. It can have clear authority. It can be ready for execution. And it can have been correct at the moment it was made.

But none of these conditions makes the context immutable.

Organisations must be able to distinguish between the original validity of a decision and the persistence of the conditions that support it. Monitoring assumptions does not mean continuously searching for new reasons to change. It means recognising that execution takes place in a reality capable of altering what we considered sufficiently true.

RATIO Principle #20
The decision can remain the same.
The assumptions can change.
Judgement must know how to recognise it.

A responsible decision does not require only the courage to be made. It also requires the capacity to recognise when the conditions that supported it deserve to be re-examined.

Question for the Board
Think of an important strategic decision your organisation is already executing.

You probably know its objectives. You know the responsibilities. You have indicators that measure its results.

But can you identify the three most important assumptions on which that decision was built? Do you know what information could call them into question?

And above all: if one of these assumptions stopped being valid tomorrow, who would notice, and who would be responsible for bringing that information to the attention of the decision maker?

If the answer is not clear, there may be a risk that ordinary outcome indicators are not yet showing.
