The Most Dangerous Risk Is Solving the Wrong Problem Well

Many executive decisions do not fail because the reasoning is weak. They fail because the organisation has developed an excellent answer to a question it should never have asked.

An excellent answer does not correct a flawed question.

A decision can be flawless in its execution and profoundly flawed in its origin.

Organisations invest considerable resources in the quality of their decisions.

They analyse data.

They compare alternatives.

They engage specialists.

They assess risk.

They build business cases.

Then they decide.

But there is a question that is asked far less often:

Are we deciding on the right problem?

This distinction appears straightforward.

In practice, it separates two fundamentally different standards of decision quality.

The first concerns the ability to choose well among available options.

The second concerns something earlier and considerably harder:

establishing whether those options actually belong to the problem the organisation should be solving.

A board can have excellent data, capable leaders, rigorous analysis, and a disciplined process.

It can select the best available option.

And still be wrong.

Because a decision can be correct with respect to the question posed and mistaken with respect to the problem that actually exists.

The decision begins before the options

By the time a decision reaches the executive table, it rarely arrives as neutral reality.

It has already been interpreted.

Someone observed a signal.

That signal received an explanation.

The explanation became a problem statement.

The problem statement generated a set of possible solutions.

Only at that point does the room formally begin to decide.

And it is precisely here that one of the most underestimated vulnerabilities in organisational decision-making takes root.

The discussion may focus entirely on the quality of the solutions without ever revisiting the quality of the initial problem definition.

The risk is not necessarily shallow analysis.

It may be the opposite.

An organisation can analyse the wrong problem with extraordinary rigour.

And the better the subsequent analysis, the more convincing the decision becomes.

A decision can be correct with respect to the question posed and mistaken with respect to the problem that actually exists.

The problem that seemed self-evident

Consider an organisation whose commercial growth is decelerating.

The signal is real.

The board takes note.

Within a short period, an explanation surfaces:

commercial leadership is underperforming.

The explanation becomes a diagnosis.

The diagnosis becomes a requirement:

a new Chief Commercial Officer is needed.

From this point, the organisation can execute everything correctly.

Define an excellent job specification.

Engage executive search.

Identify high-calibre candidates.

Run rigorous assessments.

Compare track records.

Evaluate leadership, execution capability, and strategic range.

Ultimately select the strongest candidate.

But there is a prior question that may never have been genuinely examined:

Is the commercial underperformance actually caused by commercial leadership?

It might be pricing.

Product.

Market positioning.

Distribution.

A structural shift in buyer behaviour.

An operational constraint limiting the sales function's capacity to convert.

A value proposition the market no longer considers sufficiently differentiated.

If any of these interpretations were closer to the truth, the organisation would have just executed an excellent decision-making process around the wrong problem.

That is not an execution failure.

It is a framing error.

Realistic Scenario

A company posts three consecutive quarters of growth below expectations.

The first layer of analysis identifies a decline in commercial performance.

The executive conversation rapidly shifts to the leadership of the Sales function.

Within weeks, the question has quietly changed. It is no longer:

"Why is growth slowing?"

It has become:

"Who should be leading commercial differently?"

This shift appears minor.

It is not.

The first question investigates the problem.

The second presumes the cause is already known.

From that point forward, every subsequent analysis risks becoming a sophisticated search for confirmation of a conclusion reached too soon.

From signal to decision

Before an organisation begins comparing alternatives, it is useful to make visible the pathway through which a signal has been transformed into a decision.

Conflating these four levels means allowing an interpretation to solidify progressively into organisational reality — without ever being tested.

The signal is what we observe.

The interpretation is what we believe it means.

The problem is what we decide to address.

The decision is what we do as a result.

The fact that a problem has been articulated with clarity does not mean it has been articulated correctly.

When experience amplifies the risk

Executive experience is indispensable.

It enables rapid pattern recognition.

It filters noise.

It accelerates interpretation.

It allows leaders to connect present situations to hundreds of situations they have navigated before.

But precisely this capability can become a source of vulnerability.

A familiar pattern produces a familiar explanation quickly.

And a familiar explanation can feel true before it has been tested.

The experienced executive sees a commercial slowdown and recognises a situation they have encountered before.

The difficulty is that recognising a resemblance is not the same as establishing a cause.

Two situations can produce identical signals and have entirely different origins.

Experience improves judgement when it generates hypotheses.

It becomes dangerous when it closes them down too quickly.

Experience should accelerate the generation of hypotheses, not their closure.

The RATIO Problem Frame Test

Before a room begins debating which decision to take, an executive team should be capable of separating what it knows from what it is interpreting.

Question What It Tests

Verifies that the starting point is a genuine signal, not an explanation already embedded in the language being used.
02

The cost of precision applied to the wrong problem

Organisations tend to treat precision as an unconditional virtue.

It is not.

Precision creates value only when applied to the right question.

A financial model can be impeccable.

An executive search process can be impeccable.

An assessment can be impeccable.

A strategy can be impeccable.

But none of these instruments can automatically correct an error that occurred before they were used.

The further an organisation moves into the execution of a decision, the more psychologically and operationally difficult it becomes to step back and interrogate the original problem definition.

The budget has been committed.

The teams have been mobilised.

The stakeholders have been aligned.

The narrative has been communicated.

The decision has acquired institutional momentum.

At that point, the initial framing is no longer a hypothesis.

It has become infrastructure.

Which is precisely why decision quality must be protected before commitment is made.

Decision Stress Test

Before the Next High-Stakes Decision

Before the room begins comparing alternatives, ask these questions:

→ What exactly is the fact we are trying to explain?
→ Which part of our problem definition is evidence — and which is interpretation?
→ What are three other causes that could produce the same signal?
→ What information would cause us to reframe the problem?
→ If we removed our preferred solution, would we still define the problem the same way?
→ Are we finding the best decision — or are we verifying that a decision is actually necessary?

Do not begin with the solution. First verify which problem makes it necessary.

Decision Intelligence begins with framing

Decision Intelligence does not exist merely to improve the comparison of alternatives.

It intervenes earlier.

It makes visible the pathway through which a signal becomes a problem — and a problem becomes a decision.

This matters because organisations do not decide on reality directly.

They decide on the representation of reality they have constructed.

The quality of that representation defines the field within which all subsequent reasoning will operate.

A poorly framed problem constrains the available options.

A problem framed too quickly conceals competing interpretations.

A problem framed around a preferred solution makes the decision feel almost inevitable.

This is why sound decision architecture does not only ask:

"What is the best option?"

It asks first:

"What leads us to believe this is the right problem?"

Before the next important decision

Before the next executive hire, strategic investment, organisational restructuring, market entry, or board decision — pausing briefly on the framing can be worth more than hours spent subsequently evaluating alternatives.

Ask:

What is the signal?

What is our interpretation?

What is the problem?

Which step between these three elements have we actually verified?

Because a decision does not improve simply by increasing the quality of the analysis.

It improves when the analysis begins from the right problem.

Mature organisations do not only ask whether they are making the right decision.
They ask whether they are deciding on the right problem.

The quality of a decision is inseparable from the quality of the problem that precedes it.

Organisations that consistently produce better decisions are not those that always find the answer quickly.

They are those that hold the question open long enough to verify which problem genuinely warrants an answer.

RATIO introduces discipline into the phase that precedes commitment — making visible the assumptions, interpretations, and decisional risks before they solidify into choices that are difficult to reverse.

Before improving the answer, verify the question.

Question for the Board
When a consequential decision reaches your executive table, how much time is spent selecting the solution —
and how much is spent verifying that the problem that solution is meant to address is actually the right one?

If the problem definition cannot be clearly separated from the facts that generated it, the room may not yet be deciding.
It may simply be operationalising an interpretation.
