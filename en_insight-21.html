An organisation can make individually rational decisions and unknowingly build excessive overall exposure. Risk does not depend only on the quality of individual choices, but also on the assumptions, resources and vulnerabilities those choices share.

Five good decisions can create a problem

A board of directors approves an international expansion.

A chief executive authorises an acquisition.

The chief financial officer backs a new investment programme.

The chief operating officer launches an industrial transformation.

The HR function proposes strengthening the management team.

Every initiative is examined.

The analyses are consistent. The assumptions are discussed. The risks are assessed. The responsibilities are assigned.

Each decision, considered individually, appears rational.

And yet the organisation may have created a risk that none of the individual assessments has made sufficiently visible.

All the initiatives may depend on the same growth scenario.

They may draw on the same financial resources.

They may require the attention of the same people.

They may be vulnerable to the same market shift.

The risk is not necessarily inside a specific decision.

It can emerge from the relationships between individually sound decisions.

RATIO Principle
The quality of individual decisions
does not guarantee the soundness of their sum.

From the single decision to the decision portfolio

Organisations are accustomed to evaluating decisions through specific processes.

An investment is analysed from an economic standpoint. An appointment is assessed against the role. An acquisition is examined through opportunities, risks and conditions. A transformation is studied in relation to its objectives.

This discipline is necessary.

But every decision enters an organisation in which other decisions have already been made.

Resources are not infinite. Priorities can compete. Assumptions can be shared. Consequences can overlap.

We define a decision portfolio as the set of relevant strategic decisions an organisation has made or is making, considered in their mutual relationships.

This is not only a financial portfolio. It includes investments, appointments, expansions, transformations, capacity allocation and organisational choices.

RATIO — Two Levels of Analysis
01
Quality of the Single Decision
Is the choice rational relative to evidence, context, alternatives and uncertainty?
02
Soundness of the Decision Portfolio
Do the decisions, taken together, create exposures, dependencies or pressures that do not emerge from the individual assessments?

"A good decision is not automatically
a good addition to the decision portfolio."

Decision concentration risk

Imagine three strategic initiatives.

The first enters a new market. The second increases production capacity. The third acquires a competitor.

Each initiative has a convincing rationale. But all three assume sustained growth in demand within the same sector.

If that growth materialises, the decisions may reinforce one another. If demand slows, all three may be hit at the same time.

This is decision concentration risk.

It does not necessarily stem from having chosen a single initiative poorly. It stems from having built multiple commitments on a shared vulnerability.

Shared assumptions create hidden vulnerabilities

In Insight #20 we introduced decision assumption monitoring. Every decision rests on conditions we consider sufficiently valid.

But some assumptions can support numerous choices at the same time. An organisation may presuppose:

  • Stable energy costs.
  • Availability of financing.
  • Growth of a given market.
  • Continuity of regulatory conditions.
  • Availability of strategic competencies.

These hypotheses can appear in different decision dossiers without being recognised as a single source of shared exposure.

The question therefore changes. Not only: "What are the critical assumptions of this decision?" But also:

"How many other decisions depend
on the same assumptions?"

Single-decision monitoring observes whether its premises remain valid. Portfolio analysis observes how exposed the organisation is when the same condition supports multiple decisions.

Risk is not only financial

Organisations may focus on the overall amount of investment. That matters, but it is not sufficient.

Two decisions can compete for a resource that does not immediately appear in financial models. Management attention. Specialist competencies. Coordination capacity. The time of a key executive. The organisation's availability to sustain multiple transformations.

A company may possess the economic resources needed to fund five initiatives. But it may not possess the operational capacity to carry out all of them at the same time.

Each project, evaluated individually, may appear achievable. Taken together, they can exceed the organisation's actual capacity.

RATIO — Two Different Exposures
01
Financial Exposure
How much capital and liquidity do the decisions require overall?
02
Organisational Exposure
How much competency, operational capacity and management attention do they require in the same period?
RATIO Principle
Having the resources to approve more decisions
does not mean having the capacity to execute all of them.

This connects overall risk to the execution readiness introduced in Insight #19.

Decisions can reinforce or weaken one another

Not every relationship between decisions is negative.

Two initiatives can create synergies. New infrastructure can enable commercial expansion. An executive appointment can strengthen the ability to deliver a transformation. A partnership can reduce the resources needed for an investment.

But interactions can also amplify risk. Two projects may depend on the same supplier. Three initiatives may require the same competencies. One decision can reduce the flexibility needed to complete another.

It is therefore useful to distinguish three types of relationship.

RATIO — Three Types of Relationship
01
Reinforcement
A decision increases the feasibility or potential value of another.
02
Competition
Multiple decisions simultaneously require limited resources, capacity or attention.
03
Shared Vulnerability
Multiple decisions depend on the same critical condition or are exposed to the same event.

Portfolio assessment must consider all three. It is not enough to look for common risks. It is also necessary to understand when choices create value together and when they come into conflict.

Simultaneity can change the risk

A corporate transformation can be sustainable. A second transformation can be equally sustainable. But carrying them out at the same time can produce a different pressure from executing them separately.

Time becomes a variable of the portfolio. An organisation may have approved correct initiatives, but concentrated their execution in the same period.

This can increase: the demand for resources; organisational dependencies; competition between priorities; the difficulty of coordination; exposure to sudden changes.

The question is not only which decisions to make. It is also which decisions to activate together, which to sequence, and which to subordinate to specific conditions.

RATIO Principle
Individually sustainable decisions can become
collectively fragile when activated at the same time.

The timeline can change the risk without necessarily changing the content of the individual choices.

An executive scenario: three strategies, one vulnerability

A European industrial group decides to accelerate growth. The board approves three initiatives.

First decision: acquire a competitor to increase market share.

Second decision: expand production capacity.

Third decision: enter a new geographic area.

The individual analyses are convincing. The acquisition offers potential synergies. The production expansion responds to commercial forecasts. The new market offers growth opportunities.

But the portfolio reveals certain concentrations.

All the initiatives depend on sufficiently strong sector demand. The acquisition and the production expansion absorb a significant share of financial capacity. Two projects require the competencies of the same leadership group. Entry into the new market depends on production capacity that the second initiative has yet to develop.

A slowdown in demand could therefore simultaneously hit the expected value of all three decisions. A delay in production could also compromise the commercial path. And excessive organisational pressure could reduce the quality of execution.

None of these observations proves that the three decisions are wrong. But their overall risk may be different from what is perceived by examining them one at a time.

Decision Question
Can the group sustain the overall exposure?

The solution does not necessarily consist in cancelling one of the initiatives. It could consist in changing their sequence, preserving available capacity, introducing activation conditions, or reassessing the intensity of the commitments.

The value of the analysis lies in making the problem visible before it becomes a consequence.

Overall risk is not a simple sum

Talking about a decision portfolio does not mean claiming that total risk is always equal to the sum of individual risks.

Interactions can produce different effects. Two initiatives can offset each other. They can share resources and create economies. They can reduce overall exposure through different activities. Or they can mutually amplify vulnerabilities.

RATIO — Three Possibilities
01
Compensation
The characteristics of the decisions reduce certain common exposures.
02
Accumulation
Multiple decisions increase exposure to the same risk factor.
03
Amplification
Interdependencies can make the consequences of an event more extensive than isolated assessments suggest.
RATIO Principle
Portfolio risk is not always the sum of risks.
It is also the result of the relationships between decisions.

The quality of judgement must therefore extend from evaluating individual choices to understanding their interactions.

Concentrating decisions is not always wrong

One possible misunderstanding must be avoided. Risk concentration is not automatically a bad choice.

An organisation can consciously decide to concentrate resources and attention on a strategic priority. That can be a rational choice. An important transformation may require coordinated investments. A new platform may require numerous complementary decisions. A strategy can generate value precisely because the initiatives converge toward a common objective.

The problem is not concentration itself. It is concentration that is unrecognised, unassessed, or incompatible with the organisation's capacity to sustain its consequences.

CONSCIOUS CONCENTRATION ≠ INVISIBLE CONCENTRATION

The question is not: "Should we always distribute risk?" It is:

"Do we understand what exposure we are building,
and why we consider it rational to sustain it?"

Who holds the overall view?

Insight #17 introduced decision accountability. Every decision should have recognisable authority.

But when decisions belong to different functions, a problem can emerge.

The finance director knows the risk of the investment. The operations director knows the risk of the transformation. The commercial head knows the risk of the expansion. Each function observes part of the reality.

Who observes the shared exposures?

This does not mean a new centre of power must be created. And it does not mean transferring authority away from existing governance structures. It means ensuring that the information needed for overall assessment reaches the parties who hold the appropriate mandate.

RATIO Principle
Having an owner for every decision does not guarantee
that someone is observing the relationships between decisions.

Individual accountability and overall visibility are both important.

Artificial intelligence can analyse relationships. Not guarantee judgement.

Artificial intelligence can help identify recurring assumptions, dependencies and concentrations. It can compare decision documents. It can identify resources required by multiple initiatives. It can build scenarios showing how a change might affect several decisions.

This can improve overall visibility. But a limit exists.

The presence of a relationship in the data does not automatically demonstrate its strategic importance. Two decisions may share a variable without being exposed in the same way. Two apparently independent initiatives may have dependencies not represented in the data. A model may suggest a concentration without knowing all the organisation's real constraints.

DETECTING A RELATIONSHIP ≠ UNDERSTANDING ITS RISK

Artificial intelligence can help represent the portfolio. The meaning of the relationships must be interpreted through context, evidence and judgement.

Executive hiring: appointments also form a system

Portfolio risk does not concern investments only. It also concerns decisions about the people who lead the organisation.

A company can appoint three excellent executives. Each candidate may prove consistent with their role. But the three appointments may depend on the same conditions.

A corporate transformation not yet completed. Resources that must be shared. Operational mandates that overlap. Board expectations that are not fully compatible.

The individual quality of the candidates does not automatically solve these problems. Three correct appointments, assessed separately, can run into difficulty if the roles come into conflict, or if the organisation lacks the capacity needed to sustain them simultaneously.

This reinforces the RATIO principle:

RATIO Principle
Role First.
Then the Candidate.

But it adds a dimension. Before even evaluating the interaction between a person and their role, it may be necessary to understand how that role interacts with other strategic mandates.

A SET OF GOOD APPOINTMENTS DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY GUARANTEE A COHERENT LEADERSHIP STRUCTURE

RATIO operates in the territory of executive decision intelligence. Its objective is to improve the visibility of the reasoning that precedes significant choices.

The decision portfolio introduces a further perspective. Not only the quality of the single decision, but the relationships between multiple decisions that can influence the organisation's overall risk.

This does not turn RATIO into an investment manager. It does not mean automatically assigning a numerical value to aggregate risk. It does not replace the board, company leadership or control functions. It means recognising that some vulnerabilities can emerge only when decisions are observed together.

"RATIO does not decide on behalf of leaders.
It makes more visible the conditions under which their judgement is exercised."

The final principle

A decision can be rational. It can have explicit assumptions. It can be supported by credible evidence. It can have passed a critical review. It can have clear accountability. It can be ready for execution. And it can remain valid as the context evolves.

But none of these qualities guarantees that its inclusion in the organisation's overall portfolio does not introduce new vulnerabilities.

The single decision can be sound. The system of decisions can be fragile. Risk can concentrate in shared assumptions, contested resources and unrecognised dependencies.

For this reason, the quality of executive judgement must be able to observe two levels. The decision we are making. And the organisation we are building through all the decisions already made.

RATIO Principle #21
A decision can be sound.
Its portfolio can be fragile.
Risk also emerges from the relationships between choices.

The problem is not only deciding well, once. It is understanding how our decisions, together, are shaping the organisation's future and exposure.

Question for the Board
Think of the five most important strategic decisions currently being executed in your organisation.

Each probably has a rationale. Each has received approvals. Each has objectives and responsibilities.

But have you ever examined all five together? Do they share the same economic assumptions? Do they depend on the same resources? Do they require the attention of the same executives? Could they be compromised by the same shift in context?

And above all: if a single critical assumption stopped being valid tomorrow, how many of your strategic decisions would become vulnerable at the same time?

If the answer is not clear, there may be an exposure that no individual analysis has yet made visible.
