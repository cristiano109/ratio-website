When Everyone Participates in the Decision,
but No One Owns It.

Consensus may be broad, information may be complete, and the process may appear rigorous. But if it is not clear who has the authority to decide, accountability risks dissolving precisely when it is needed most.

Executive decisions are rarely made in isolation. A CEO listens to the CFO, CHRO, COO and the heads of different business units. The Board examines analyses, scenarios and recommendations. Business functions contribute with different competencies and perspectives. This plurality is a resource. But participation is not the same as authority. And consensus is not the same as accountability.

The paradox of collective decisions

An organisation can involve many people, gather solid evidence and reach apparent agreement without ever clarifying who is actually authorised to make the decision. When this happens, the process may appear inclusive and rigorous, but its architecture remains incomplete.

The question is not how many people participated. It is whether, at the end of the process, it is clear who decides, on the basis of which criteria and with what accountability.

"Shared accountability can strengthen a decision.
Undefined accountability can make it invisible."

Four roles that must not be confused

A complex decision may require many competencies, but not everyone involved plays the same function.

RATIO — Decision Ownership Architecture
01
Decision Input
Those who provide evidence, data, expertise and perspectives. An informational contribution does not automatically confer decision authority.
02
Decision Recommendation
Those who compare alternatives and formulate a reasoned proposal, making explicit the criteria, assumptions and uncertainties.
03
Decision Authority
Those who hold the formal mandate to choose among alternatives, within the limits of applicable delegations and governance rules.
04
Decision Accountability
Those who answer for assigned responsibilities, the quality of the process, documentation and subsequent review — without confusing accountability with absolute control of the outcome.

These roles can be distributed among different people or, where appropriate, concentrated in the same individual. There is no configuration valid for every organisation. The principle is that the distribution must be explicit.

Those who contribute must not automatically be mistaken for those who approve. Those who recommend do not necessarily hold final authority. Those who decide must know the boundaries of their mandate. And the responsibilities for execution and review must be assigned without ambiguity.

Consensus is not a substitute for authority

Consensus can improve the quality of a decision. It can surface different perspectives, reduce resistance and facilitate execution. But not all decisions require unanimity, and not every disagreement signals that the process should continue.

When consensus becomes an implicit and unlimited condition, every stakeholder can acquire a power of deferral that does not correspond to their formal responsibility. The decision is discussed, revised and postponed. No one declares they have blocked it, but no one is able to conclude it.

CONSULTATION ≠ APPROVAL
CONSENSUS ≠ AUTHORITY
PARTICIPATION ≠ OWNERSHIP

The point is not to reduce consultation. It is to distinguish necessary consultation from required approval and to clarify in advance who can decide when a legitimate disagreement persists.

The cost of ambiguous authority

In the previous Insight we introduced Decision Latency: the time that separates a materially decidable decision from the organisation's actual commitment. Ambiguity of authority can be one of its primary causes.

A proposal moves from one function to another. Each passage produces new comments, but not necessarily new evidence. A committee defers to management. Management awaits committee alignment. The process continues to move without approaching a choice.

The cost is not only the time spent in meetings. It can include the loss of alternatives, uncertainty for teams, the weakening of priorities and the progressive change of context. The organisation may interpret this movement as prudence, when part of the delay actually derives from a governance problem.

Decision Ownership does not mean deciding alone

Assigning an owner does not mean concentrating all competencies in one person, ignoring the Board or bypassing controls. It means identifying the individual who, within applicable delegations, has the responsibility to bring the process to its decision point.

An effective owner does not eliminate dissent: they organise its contribution. They do not substitute evidence with their own authority: they verify that relevant evidence has been considered. They do not demand certainty: they make residual uncertainty explicit.

Ownership concerns the clarity of the architecture, not individual prominence.

An executive scenario

An international group must decide whether to launch an operational transformation involving multiple business units. The COO supports the initiative based on identified inefficiencies. The CFO requests further economic verification. The CHRO highlights organisational risks. Business unit heads express different priorities.

All contributions are legitimate. The problem emerges when no one has clarified which opinions are advisory, which approvals are mandatory, and who holds the final decision.

After several meetings, the project has been neither approved nor rejected. Each function waits for another to take the initiative. Meanwhile, economic and operational conditions change.

The solution does not necessarily consist of another meeting. What is needed is to clarify the decision mandate, distinguish evidence still required from individual function preferences, and establish how to resolve remaining disagreements.

"Who holds the authority to conclude this decision,
and what conditions must be met before they can exercise it?"

Decision Accountability is not Outcome Guarantee

A distinction is essential to keep this Insight consistent with the principle introduced in Insight #14: Decision Quality is not the same as Outcome Quality.

Those who own a decision must answer for their mandate, compliance with applicable rules, the quality of reasoning and the transparency of assumptions. But they cannot guarantee that external events, probabilities and uncertainty will always produce the desired result.

A negative outcome does not automatically prove that the owner decided poorly. A positive outcome does not prove that governance was adequate. To evaluate accountability correctly, it is necessary to reconstruct what was known, the alternatives available, the criteria used and the limits of the authority exercised.

"Accountability requires an answerable decision process,
not a promise of a certain outcome."

The RATIO Decision Ownership Gate

Before initiating a high-impact decision, the organisation can verify seven conditions.

RATIO — Decision Ownership Gate
01
Decision
What is the concrete decision to be made?
02
Owner
Who has the mandate to lead the process to a choice?
03
Authority
Who holds the formal authority to approve or decide?
04
Input
Which people and functions must contribute with evidence and perspectives?
05
Rights
Which approvals, delegations and limits must be respected?
06
Escalation
How is a disagreement resolved that cannot be resolved at the current level?
07
Accountability
Who documents the reasoning, coordinates subsequent responsibilities and ensures the planned review?

This gate is not an automatic decision system. It is a verification of organisational clarity. The answers may vary based on structure, the nature of the decision and the organisation's governance rules.

From Decision Traceability to traceable accountability

Insight #13 introduced Decision Traceability: preserving the logic that existed when the decision was made. The same discipline must include the architecture of responsibilities.

A useful decision record does not only preserve the problem, evidence, assumptions, alternatives and criteria. It also identifies who contributed, who formulated the recommendation, who exercised authority and what responsibilities were assigned for execution and review.

This traceability does not serve to build a retrospective search for blame. It serves to make the process reconstructable, verifiable and capable of learning. The connection with Decision Reassessment is direct: without a clear trace of original responsibilities, reassessing a decision risks becoming an exercise in memory rather than analysis.

RATIO examines the quality of reasoning that precedes executive decisions. The clarity of Decision Ownership represents a complementary organisational dimension: it helps understand whether the process possesses the necessary conditions for informed judgement to translate into an effective decision.

RATIO does not replace the authority of leaders, does not assume the role of corporate governance bodies and does not decide on behalf of the organisation. Its objective is to make the quality of the decision-making process more visible and assessable.

The final principle

A decision can involve many people without losing clarity. It can value dissent without becoming interminable. It can distribute expertise without dispersing responsibility.

But this requires an explicit distinction between contributing, recommending, deciding and answering for assigned responsibilities.

When these functions remain indistinct, the organisation may obtain an apparently shared process that is incapable of concluding. When they are clear, collaboration can strengthen judgement without making authority invisible.

Board Question
Think of a strategic decision currently open in your organisation.

Everyone knows who to consult.

But is it equally clear who has the mandate to decide, which approvals are required, and who is accountable for the process after the choice is made?

If the answer requires a new meeting to be defined, the problem may not be a lack of information.

It may be a lack of Decision Ownership.
