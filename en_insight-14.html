A good decision can produce a bad outcome.
And vice versa.

Organisations frequently evaluate the quality of a decision by looking at the outcome it produced. This is dangerous. Decision quality and outcome quality are not the same thing.

Outcome Bias

Insight #13 introduced Decision Traceability: the discipline of preserving the reasoning that existed at the moment a decision was made, before the outcome had the chance to rewrite it. This Insight advances that argument toward a consequence that organisations rarely confront with sufficient rigour. Even when the trace exists, we need to know what to look for within it. And the answer requires separating, with precision, two concepts that organisational practice tends to conflate.

Decision quality.

Outcome quality.

They are not the same thing.

They are not necessarily correlated.

And conflating them is one of the most common mechanisms through which organisations draw the wrong lessons from their own experience.

The fundamental distinction

A high-quality decision does not guarantee a positive outcome.

A positive outcome does not demonstrate that the decision was high quality.

Between a decision and its result lies territory that no organisation controls entirely: uncertainty, context, external variables, timing, the people involved, unforeseeable events. A well-constructed decision — grounded in sound evidence, explicit assumptions, alternatives genuinely considered, risks identified, and clear criteria — increases the probability of a desirable outcome. It cannot guarantee one. The world produces outcomes. The decision produces only the best available conditions for the desired result to become more probable.

Decision Quality asks:

What did we know at the time of the decision?

What did we not know?

Which assumptions were we making?

What evidence supported the choice?

Which alternatives were genuinely considered?

Which uncertainties remained open?

Were the criteria explicit?

Was the reasoning coherent given the information available then?

Outcome Quality asks:

What happened?

These questions must remain separate before they are connected.

"A decision answers the question: was it justified to decide this way, given what we knew?
An outcome answers a different question: what happened in the real world?"

Outcome Bias

There is a precise mechanism that makes this separation difficult. It is called Outcome Bias.

It occurs when we judge the quality of a past decision primarily through knowledge of its eventual result — not through an analysis of the process that preceded it, not through a reconstruction of the information available at the time, not through an evaluation of the reasoning quality. But through the outcome.

The mechanism is automatic. When we know something went well, the process that generated it appears more robust than it was. When we know something went badly, that process appears weaker than it was.

In organisations, Outcome Bias produces concrete and costly consequences:

It rewards weak judgement that happened to produce a positive result.

It penalises sound judgement that happened to produce a negative result.

It builds executive reputations on outcomes rather than decision-making competence.

It feeds the wrong learning: organisations replicate what worked without understanding why it worked; they abandon what failed without understanding whether the problem was in the decision or in the world.

Deserved Success — the replicability quadrant

A good decision produced a good outcome. The process was sound and reality confirmed it.

The correct organisational response is not to celebrate the result. It is to understand precisely which elements of the decision-making process contributed to the quality of the judgement — which evidence was available and how it was weighted, how uncertainties were managed, which criteria guided the choice between alternatives.

Without this analysis, success becomes an imprecise source of learning. The organisation knows it worked. It does not know why it worked. And therefore it does not know with precision what to replicate.

Unlucky Decision — the sound judgement quadrant

A good decision produced a bad outcome. The process was sound. The evidence had been properly evaluated. The assumptions were reasonable. The uncertainty had been identified. But reality produced an adverse result.

This happens. Uncertainty is not a methodological error. It is a permanent condition of any consequential decision.

The wrong organisational response is to dismantle the decision-making process on the basis of the outcome. The correct response is to distinguish: was the process adequate? If so, the problem was not in the judgement. It was in the world.

Organisations that systematically penalise good decisions with bad outcomes end up developing risk aversion where methodological rigour should be. Their leaders learn to protect their reputations rather than improve the quality of their reasoning.

Dangerous Luck — the most dangerous quadrant

⚠ Attention — Dangerous Luck

A weak decision produced a positive result.

The assumptions were fragile. The evidence was insufficient. The alternatives were not genuinely considered. The reasoning was opaque. But the outcome was positive.

This is the most dangerous quadrant in the entire matrix. Not because the result is negative — it is positive. But because success prevents scrutiny of the process. And when a weak process goes unexamined, its flaws become organisational habits.

Unverified assumptions get replicated. Non-rigorous reasoning gets consolidated. Luck gets mistaken for competence. The organisation develops false confidence in its decision-making capability.

The next decision with the same process characteristics may produce a very different result. But no one will know until it is too late to intervene on the method.

The most difficult decisions to review are the ones that worked. There are no negative consequences. No external pressures. No immediate incentives for methodological reflection. But it is precisely in this silence that risk accumulates.

"The rear-view mirror of success is the most common blind spot in high-stakes decision environments."

Deserved Failure — the quadrant of visible accountability

A weak decision produced a bad outcome. This is the most visible quadrant. The failure is evident. The pressure to identify responsibility is high.

The correct organisational response is not to find someone to blame. It is to identify precisely where the decision-making process failed: at which stage the evidence was insufficient, at which point assumptions were not made explicit, which alternatives were not considered, which uncertainty was not acknowledged.

The risk in this quadrant is the opposite of Dangerous Luck: over-correction. Organisations can develop hyperburocratic processes or decision paralysis in response to a failure whose problem was localised to a specific point in the reasoning.

Decision Traceability makes this precision possible: instead of judging the entire decision architecture on the basis of the result, one can isolate the exact point where the process weakened.

An executive scenario

An organisation must choose between two candidates for a critical leadership role.

Candidate A has a stronger historical performance record, but analysis of the available evidence signals lower contextual fit with the organisation's future operating environment.

Candidate B has less conventional experience, but demonstrates stronger alignment with the conditions in which the role will need to operate over the next three years.

Leadership selects Candidate B after explicitly evaluating the evidence, assumptions, risks and uncertainties. The process is documented. The reasoning is defensible.

Six months later, an external restructuring makes the operating environment very different from what had been anticipated. The appointment does not produce the expected results.

The wrong question is: "Did the candidate succeed?"

The right question is: "Given what we knew at the time, was the reasoning behind the decision sound?"

Then three elements are examined separately:

What was foreseeable given the available information.

What was uncertain but identifiable as such.

What was genuinely unknowable.

Only this distinction makes real learning possible — not about the appointment, but about the quality of the judgement, and about where the decision-making process can be strengthened for the next consequential decision.

The most dangerous decisions may be the ones that worked

Every organisation carries a set of decisions in its history that are never reviewed. Not because they were irrelevant. But because they worked.

Success interrupts the review process. There are no negative consequences. No external pressures. No immediate incentives for methodological reflection.

But some of these decisions sit in the Dangerous Luck quadrant. The outcome was positive. The process was not necessarily so. And the weaknesses of that process — the unverified assumptions, the alternatives not considered, the uncertainties ignored — have become over time the foundations on which subsequent decisions are built.

When context shifts enough to expose these weaknesses, organisations are surprised. Not because the world changed suddenly. But because they had built on the sand of a decision that appeared solid.

The hidden risk is not in the failure that is visible. It is in the success that goes unexamined.

From Performance Review to Decision Review

Organisations have established processes for reviewing results: performance reviews, post-mortems, retrospectives, KPI analysis. These instruments measure what happened. Far less often does an equivalent process exist for reviewing the architecture of the decisions that preceded those results.

A quality Decision Review does not ask: "Did it work?"

It asks a different set of questions.

01
What did we expect?
What was the anticipated outcome at the time of the decision? Was it defined with sufficient precision to be verifiable?
02
What actually happened?
What is the gap between expected and actual outcome — in the right dimension, in the right direction, within the anticipated timeframe?
03
Which assumptions proved correct?
Among the explicit assumptions at the time of the decision, which did reality confirm? What does this reinforce about the process?
04
Which assumptions proved wrong?
Which hypotheses did not hold? Were they identifiably weak at the time, or were they reasonable given the information then available?
05
Which uncertainties materialised?
Among the risks that had been identified, which occurred? Were they correctly assessed in terms of probability and impact?
06
What did we genuinely know at the time?
Separate rigorously what was available before the decision from what only became visible afterwards.
07
Would we make the same decision with the same information?
This is the final question. It answers decision quality, not outcome quality.

RATIO operates in the space that precedes the moment consequential decisions become outcomes.

The objective is not certainty. The objective is stronger, more visible, more reviewable reasoning — independent of the outcome that will follow.

Because when the decision trace exists, it becomes possible to separate what was controllable from what was not. And to build learning that is real, not merely fortunate.

The principle that separates learning from luck

A good outcome does not prove that the decision was good.

A bad outcome does not prove that the decision was bad.

Organisations become better decision-makers when they learn to distinguish five separate things.

01
What we decided
02
Why we decided it
03
What we expected
04
What actually happened
05
What we learned

These five dimensions never collapse into one.

Keeping them separate is the necessary condition for turning experience into genuine decision-making competence.

Question for the Board
Take the last consequential decision in your organisation that produced a positive result.

Can you answer with certainty:
was the decision-making process sound — or was the result assisted by variables you did not control?

If the answer requires reconstructing evidence, assumptions and criteria that were never made explicit, you are looking at a result. Not necessarily at competence.

Deserved success and dangerous luck can look identical from the outside.
