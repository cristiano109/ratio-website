A decision is not complete when it is made.
It becomes complete when it can be executed.

A decision can be rational, well-documented and formally approved. But without the conditions to translate it into action, risk is not eliminated — it changes form.

A decision can be rational, grounded in solid evidence and formally approved. But approval is not execution. Between choice and action lies a distance — made of capabilities, resources, responsibilities and conditions — that organisations do not always measure with the same rigour applied to strategic judgement.

The decision has been approved. But is it truly ready?

A board of directors approves a strategy. A chief executive authorises an investment. An organisation decides to enter a new market.

The decision has been made. But resources may not be available, operational responsibilities may be undefined and some essential conditions may not yet be met.

Organisations devote considerable attention to choosing the best alternative. Far less often do they verify with the same rigour whether they actually possess the conditions to implement it.

A decision can be rational and still encounter significant obstacles in execution, without having been necessarily wrong.

"A decision can be approved without yet being ready for execution.
The quality of judgement does not automatically create the organisational capacity to act."

Three realities not to confuse

A strategic decision passes through three distinct conditions.

RATIO — The Three Conditions
01
Decision Commitment
The organisation has chosen and authorised an alternative.
What have we decided?
02
Execution Readiness
The necessary conditions to proceed are either available or adequately in place.
Are we in a position to act?
03
Execution Outcomes
The organisation observes what actually happens after activation.
What is our action actually producing?
DECISION COMMITMENT ≠ EXECUTION READINESS
EXECUTION READINESS ≠ EXECUTION SUCCESS

An approved decision does not demonstrate that the organisation is ready. Being ready does not guarantee success. A negative outcome, moreover, does not automatically prove that the original decision was irrational.

Execution readiness constitutes a distinct dimension of decision risk.

The gap between decision and action

We define the decision-to-action gap as the distance between a formally made decision and the organisation's capacity to concretely begin its implementation.

This is not merely a temporal distance. It may depend on capabilities, resources, coordination, responsibilities or conditions that are still missing.

"Risk does not end at approval. It can change form in the transition to action."

A strategy is not yet an action

"We will enter two new international markets."

The strategy may be clear, but its operational conditions are not necessarily so. Which market to address first? What capabilities are required? Who will lead the initiative? What authorisations are needed? What resources are available?

Communication disseminates a decision. Operational translation clarifies what must happen to realise it.

COMMUNICATING A DECISION ≠ MAKING IT EXECUTABLE

It is not necessary to define every detail before approval. It is necessary to understand the essential conditions before taking on significant commitments.

The authority to decide does not create the capacity to execute

Insight #17 clarified the importance of decision authority and accountability. But whoever decides is not necessarily the one who coordinates execution.

A board of directors may approve a strategy. The chief executive may receive the mandate to implement it. A chief operating officer may coordinate its realisation. The problem arises when the transition from decision authority to operational mandate remains implicit.

DECISION RESPONSIBILITY: who holds the authority to choose
EXECUTION RESPONSIBILITY: who holds the operational mandate
CLEAR DECISION AUTHORITY ≠ CAPACITY TO EXECUTE

Responsibilities may be distributed, but they must remain coherent from choice to action.

Approved resources are not always available

An approved budget does not immediately make the required capabilities available. A purchased technology is not automatically integrated. A new organisational structure is not necessarily already operational.

A distinction must be made:

ASSIGNED ≠ AVAILABLE
APPROVED ≠ MOBILISED
PLANNED ≠ READY

The distance between expected availability and actual availability must be recognised before it becomes a surprise during execution.

Not all dependencies carry the same weight

An investment may depend on authorisations, capabilities, contracts or technology integrations. Not every dependency, however, requires halting everything.

CRITICAL DEPENDENCIES: conditions indispensable to responsibly activate a phase
MANAGEABLE DEPENDENCIES: conditions resolvable progressively within defined limits

As shown in Insight #16, waiting too can have a cost. Execution readiness must not become a new justification for indefinite postponement.

"Not all dependencies must be resolved before acting.
Every critical dependency must be understood."

An executive scenario: international expansion

A European industrial group approves entry into two international markets. The strategy appears compelling, demand is growing and economic analyses indicate an interesting opportunity. The board approves and the chief executive communicates the decision.

During the launch, however, certain conditions emerge: the regional head has not been appointed, some regulatory requirements need to be verified, systems integration requires more time and certain capabilities are engaged elsewhere.

The strategy is not necessarily wrong. The organisation is, however, not ready to activate all the planned phases. It can proceed by ignoring the constraints, suspend all activities, or distinguish what is immediately executable from what requires further prerequisites.

The third option allows the strategic commitment to be confirmed, preparatory activities to begin and subsequent phases to be made conditional on explicit prerequisites.

Three states of execution readiness

RATIO — Readiness States
01
Ready
The essential conditions for the planned phase are met or adequately managed. Activation may begin.
02
Conditionally Ready
Some prerequisites still need to be met. Permitted activities proceed while others remain conditional on defined requirements.
03
Not Ready
Essential conditions are missing. Constraints must be resolved, the path modified or the decision reassessed if conditions materially alter its logic.

These categories are not automated certifications or scores. They help make organisational preparedness visible.

"Execution readiness does not guarantee success. It makes clear the conditions necessary to act."

The RATIO Execution Readiness Verification

Before transforming an executive decision into a significant operational commitment, seven conditions are examined.

RATIO — Execution Readiness Verification
01
Operational Translation
Has the decision been translated into understandable objectives, responsibilities and actions?
02
Execution Responsibility
Who holds the operational mandate? Are responsibilities of the functions involved clearly defined?
03
Capabilities
Are the required skills and resources available, or does a credible path to obtaining them exist?
04
Dependencies
Which conditions prevent activation? Which can be managed during the process?
05
Coordination
Do the functions involved understand priorities, interdependencies and constraints?
06
Activation Conditions
When can we proceed? Which circumstances require a pause, new authorisation or reassessment?
07
First Action and Feedback
What is the first executable step? What signals will allow us to recognise obstacles or deviations early?

This model does not assign scores and does not replace leadership judgement. It serves to make visible conditions that might otherwise remain implicit.

"Do we understand sufficiently well what separates this decision from the concrete possibility of executing it?"

Readiness must not become paralysis

Demanding perfect conditions before acting can make any initiative impossible. Some capabilities develop during execution. Some dependencies can only be resolved after the start.

PERFECT READINESS: demanding elimination of every uncertainty and completion of every preparation
SUFFICIENT READINESS: conditions adequately understood and managed for the next significant action

The level required depends on risk, reversibility and the consequences of the decision.

"The objective is not perfect readiness. It is sufficient readiness for the next relevant action."

Prudence must generate value, not become unjustified delay.

Artificial intelligence can generate a plan. Not guarantee its feasibility.

Artificial intelligence can translate strategies into activities, identify dependencies, compare scenarios and highlight inconsistencies. But producing a plan does not mean verifying all real conditions.

A system can propose the use of certain capabilities without knowing whether they are available. It can build a schedule without guaranteeing that authorisations will arrive on time. The formal quality of a plan can generate an illusion of readiness.

GENERATED PLAN ≠ VERIFIED CAPACITY
PROJECTED FEASIBILITY ≠ EXECUTION READINESS

Artificial intelligence can make conditions to be verified more visible. The assessment of organisational reality remains a human responsibility.

Appointing a leader does not mean creating the conditions for their success

An organisation appoints a new chief operating officer following a rigorous evaluation. The candidate is capable, the process is structured and the decision appears rational. But the role requires a complex transformation.

Does the executive have the necessary mandate? Are resources aligned with the objectives? Are the expectations of the chief executive and the board compatible? Can the organisation sustain what is being asked of it?

These questions do not replace the evaluation of the candidate. They complete its context. An excellent executive may encounter difficulties in an organisation not ready to support the mandate.

RATIO Principle
Role First.
Then the Candidate.
An excellent appointment does not automatically create a ready organisation.

The quality of the choice and the conditions for its implementation must be considered distinctly, but coherently.

From decision to execution: continuity, not confusion

Decision intelligence and operational management are connected but distinct disciplines. The former examines the quality of judgement and relevant conditions before commitment. The latter coordinates resources, activities and results during implementation.

If execution reveals information that materially alters the original assumptions, reassessment may be necessary. Not every difficulty proves an error. Not every deviation requires reopening the strategy. But some changes can alter the validity of the initial conditions.

RATIO operates in the territory of executive decision intelligence. It examines the quality of judgement, context, assumptions, evidence, alternatives, uncertainty, risk, timing, responsibilities and the critical review of reasoning.

Execution readiness adds a further question: are the conditions necessary to translate choice into action sufficiently visible before the organisation takes on significant commitments?

RATIO does not replace the board of directors, the chief executive or the operational leaders. It makes visible conditions and vulnerabilities that might otherwise only emerge after approval.

The final principle

A decision can be supported by solid evidence, explicit assumptions, credible alternatives and a rigorous process of deliberation. It can have a defined authority and be formally approved.

But one question remains: is the organisation in a position to act?

Understanding the distance between decision and action means recognising a further dimension of risk. Not to demand certainty. Not to postpone every initiative. But to distinguish what can be activated from what still requires essential conditions.

RATIO Principle #19
Deciding does not mean executing.
Approving does not mean being ready.
Readiness makes action possible.

The value of a decision depends not only on what we choose, but also on the capacity to understand what is required to transform that choice into action.

Question for the Board
Think of a strategic decision recently approved by your organisation.
