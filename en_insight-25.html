A decision can appear rational to whoever proposes it, consistent with assigned objectives and supported by measurable results. But if individual or functional incentives are not aligned with the organisation's overall interests, the decision-making process can generate risks that no one intended to create.

A decision can be rational for one function. But not for the organisation.

A sales director proposes an aggressive expansion.

Forecasts indicate a significant increase in revenue.

The project is consistent with the sales function's objectives.

Management examines the proposal.

The numbers look convincing.

But the expansion requires operational investment, new competencies, larger inventory and a significant draw on organisational capacity.

The commercial benefits may appear quickly.

The costs and consequences may instead emerge in other functions and at later moments.

No one is necessarily acting in bad faith.

The sales director may simply be disciplined in pursuing the objectives the organisation assigned to them.

The problem is something else.

What is rewarded at the individual or functional level does not always coincide with what creates the greatest value for the organisation.

RATIO Principle
A decision can be rational for whoever proposes it and generate a risk for the organisation that approves it.
Judgement quality requires understanding not only the logic of the proposal, but also the incentives that may have shaped how it was built.

Local rationality does not guarantee collective value

Organisations distribute objectives and responsibilities.

The sales function must grow revenue.

The finance function must preserve economic sustainability.

The operations function must guarantee efficiency and continuity.

Human Resources must support the availability of competencies.

Every function has its own success criteria.

This specialisation is necessary.

But it can introduce a vulnerability.

A choice that improves one function's indicator can worsen the conditions of another.

A cost reduction can improve the immediate financial result, but reduce future strategic capacity.

A sales increase can be positive, but require commercial conditions that undermine profitability.

A reduction in hiring time can satisfy an operational objective, but not guarantee a better match between person and role.

RATIO — Two Levels of Evaluation
01
Local Rationality
Is the decision consistent with the objectives of the function that proposes it?
02
Organisational Value
Is the decision consistent with the value, constraints and risk of the entire organisation?
FUNCTIONAL SUCCESS ≠ ORGANISATIONAL VALUE

This does not mean that individual functions' objectives are wrong.

It means that their combination must be governed.

Incentives are not only financial

When we talk about incentives, we often think of bonuses, rewards and variable pay.

But the incentives that influence executive judgement can take different forms.

A promotion.

Recognition from the board of directors.

The achievement of a public target.

The protection of professional reputation.

The desire to prove that a previous strategy was correct.

The desire to avoid an unpopular decision.

Or the need to present results by a given deadline.

These factors do not automatically determine improper behaviour.

But they can influence which opportunities are considered, which risks receive attention and which alternatives appear preferable.

The issue is not to eliminate incentives.

It is to understand how they can interact with the formation of judgement.

RATIO Principle
Incentives do not necessarily change the data. They can change the weight we give to the data.
A rigorous decision-making process should recognise this possibility without turning it into a judgement about personal intentions.

Its purpose is to make visible the differences between what is rewarded, what is decided and who bears the consequences.

Whoever receives the benefit is not always whoever bears the risk

One of the most important vulnerabilities arises from the asymmetry between benefits and consequences.

An executive may be evaluated on the achievement of an annual target.

But the decision required to achieve it may produce consequences over the following three years.

A function may achieve an improvement in its own indicators.

But the cost of that improvement may be transferred to another function.

A project may generate immediate recognition for whoever launches it.

While the difficulties of execution will be faced by different people.

This separation does not prove the decision is wrong.

But it requires attention.

RATIO — Three Questions
01
Who Benefits?
Who receives recognition for the result?
02
Who Bears the Risk?
Who faces the costs and possible negative consequences?
03
When?
Are benefits and consequences observed in the same period?
RATIO Principle
When benefits and consequences are distributed differently, judgement must recognise this asymmetry.

Time can distort incentives

A strategic decision can produce effects for many years.

But individual targets are often measured over shorter periods.

A quarter.

A half-year.

A financial year.

This difference can introduce a distortion.

A reduction in training investment might improve immediate costs.

But it might weaken future competencies.

An aggressive commercial policy might quickly increase revenue.

But it might generate contractual conditions that are not very sustainable.

A postponed investment might temporarily improve some indicators.

But increase operational risk in the long run.

The point is not to argue that the long term must always prevail over the short term.

Organisations must respect constraints on liquidity, sustainability and present results.

However, it is necessary to recognise when the time horizon of incentives does not coincide with that of the consequences.

SHORT-TERM OBJECTIVE ≠ SUSTAINABLE VALUE OVER TIME

Judgement quality must consider both.

The risk of optimising the wrong indicator

Indicators are necessary.

They allow progress, responsibility and results to be measured.

But every indicator represents only part of reality.

When it becomes the dominant objective, it can steer behaviours that improve the measure without improving, in the same proportion, the value it is meant to represent.

An organisation can measure the number of new customers.

But the number of customers alone does not describe their profitability or retention.

It can measure the speed of hiring.

But speed does not demonstrate the quality of onboarding.

It can measure cost reduction.

But savings do not automatically describe the effect on future capacity.

RATIO Principle
Improving an indicator does not necessarily mean improving the decision that produces it.
The problem is not measurement. It is mistaking a partial representation of success for overall success.

An executive scenario: growth that hides the cost

An international group wants to accelerate commercial growth.

The sales director proposes entry into a new market.

The initiative shows encouraging prospects.

The plan projects a significant increase in revenue within twelve months.

The sales function's objectives are directly tied to revenue growth.

The board of directors examines the proposal.

But a broader analysis reveals some conditions.

The expansion requires logistics investment higher than initial forecasts.

Acquiring customers quickly will require particularly favourable commercial terms.

Operational resources will be diverted from other strategic initiatives.

Actual profitability will depend on the ability to retain customers in subsequent periods.

The proposal may still be valid.

But the result rewarded in the first year does not necessarily coincide with the overall value of the initiative.

The board therefore decides to evaluate the proposal using broader criteria.

Not only revenue.

Also margins, capacity, commercial sustainability, risk and consequences for existing priorities.

It is not necessary to conclude that the sales director acted improperly.

The organisation may simply have built a system in which part of the value receives more attention than the rest.

Decision ownership does not eliminate the incentive problem

In Insight #17 we introduced decision ownership.

Who contributes?

Who recommends?

Who decides?

Who is accountable for the assigned responsibilities?

These distinctions remain fundamental.

But knowing who holds authority does not guarantee that incentives are aligned.

A decision can have a clearly identified owner and still be influenced by partial objectives.

A committee can correctly follow the approval process and evaluate information built around overly narrow criteria.

CLARITY OF AUTHORITY ≠ ALIGNMENT OF INCENTIVES

The two conditions must be observed separately.

Authority defines who holds the mandate.

Incentive alignment helps us understand which results the system encourages people to prioritise.

The link with the decision portfolio

In Insight #21 we showed that individually rational decisions can build an overall fragile portfolio.

Incentives can contribute to this phenomenon.

Each function can propose initiatives consistent with its own objectives.

Each proposal can have a convincing rationale.

But their combination can generate competition for the same resources, concentration of risk or loss of priorities.

Insight #22 also introduced the discipline of strategic trade-offs.

Every yes involves a sacrifice.

If incentives mainly reward the launch of new initiatives, the organisation may undervalue the worth of giving up non-priority opportunities.

MORE APPROVED INITIATIVES ≠ MORE VALUE CREATED

Decision quality therefore requires evaluating how local incentives influence the construction of the overall portfolio.

Incentives can also influence the information presented

An executive decision depends on the quality of the available evidence.

But the selection of information is not always neutral.

Whoever supports a proposal may give more attention to favourable elements.

They may consider certain risks less important.

They may choose a time horizon that favours the expected results.

This does not necessarily imply intentional manipulation.

People can be sincerely convinced of the validity of their own proposals.

Precisely for this reason, the decision-making process must distinguish between the interest in supporting an initiative and the quality of the evidence used.

RATIO — Three Checks
01
Contrary Evidence
What information could weaken the proposal?
02
Alternatives
Which options were considered with the same depth?
03
Evaluation Criteria
Do the chosen indicators represent the overall value, or mainly the proposing function's objectives?
RATIO Principle
The conviction of whoever proposes does not replace the verification of evidence.

Artificial intelligence can optimise an objective. Not guarantee it is the right one.

Artificial intelligence can help compare strategies, analyse indicators and identify alternatives.

It can identify paths that maximise a given result.

But the value of the analysis also depends on the objective that is assigned.

If we ask a system to maximise sales, it may propose strategies that are effective for sales.

This does not mean those strategies are necessarily the best for margins, risk or organisational sustainability.

An optimisation can be technically consistent with the problem as formulated and, at the same time, incomplete with respect to the organisation's real interests.

OPTIMISING THE OBJECTIVE ≠ CHOOSING THE RIGHT OBJECTIVE

Artificial intelligence can improve the analysis.

It does not remove the responsibility of defining what should actually be optimised and which trade-offs are acceptable.

Executive hiring can also be influenced by incentives

The issue also concerns decisions about people.

An organisation must quickly appoint a new executive.

The function responsible for selection may be evaluated on how quickly the role is filled.

Management may need to reduce an organisational gap.

The candidate may possess important competencies.

But an executive appointment should not be evaluated solely on the speed of the process.

The role might require different characteristics from those initially anticipated.

The context might not be ready.

Organisational conditions might make the appointment particularly complex.

Speed can be a legitimate objective.

But it must not replace the quality of the match between person, role and context.

This is the RATIO principle:

Principle
Role First.
Then the Candidate.

Closing a search does not automatically mean the quality of the appointment has been protected.

A rigorous process must recognise the incentives that can push toward concluding before the decision risk has been sufficiently clarified.

The verification does not assume opportunistic behaviour.

It does not replace remuneration policies or governance procedures.

"Are we rewarding results that genuinely contribute
to the value we want to protect?"

Aligning incentives does not mean eliminating every difference

Corporate functions have different responsibilities.

It is not possible, nor necessarily desirable, to assign everyone the same objectives.

The CFO must watch risks that the sales director may view from a different perspective.

The COO must protect capacity and continuity.

The board of directors must consider the overall consequences.

Diversity of perspective can improve judgement.

The problem does not arise from its existence.

It arises when the process fails to recognise and evaluate the tensions between different objectives.

RATIO Principle
Alignment does not mean uniformity.
It means making different responsibilities compatible with the overall value of the organisation.

Executive judgement must be able to use different perspectives without being automatically dominated by just one.

RATIO operates in the territory of executive decision intelligence. Its focus concerns the quality of the judgement that precedes high-impact choices.

With incentive alignment we introduce a further question: are the objectives and recognitions that influence a proposal consistent with the value and risk of the organisation as a whole?

This perspective does not turn RATIO into a compensation design system. It does not automatically attribute intentions to people. It does not replace the board of directors or control functions. It helps make visible conditions that can influence reasoning and the evaluation of alternatives. RATIO does not decide on behalf of leaders. It helps make more understandable the context in which their judgement is exercised.

The final principle

Organisations need objectives.

They need responsibility.

They need indicators that allow progress to be measured.

And they need people motivated to achieve results.

But no indicator fully represents the value of a decision.

No individual incentive automatically coincides with the organisation's overall interest.

A choice can satisfy one objective and compromise a more important priority.

It can improve a result in the short term and increase risk in the long term.

It can appear rational to one function and introduce consequences that other functions will have to bear.

The quality of executive judgement requires the ability to recognise these differences.

Not to eliminate incentives.

But to prevent them from becoming the invisible criterion through which what appears convenient is defined.

RATIO Principle #25
What is rewarded influences what is chosen.
What suits one function does not always suit the organisation.
Judgement must recognise the difference.

A quality decision must not only be consistent with the objectives of whoever proposes it.

It must be justifiable in light of the value, responsibilities and consequences of the organisation as a whole.

Question for the Board
Think of an important strategic decision your organisation is evaluating.

You probably know the expected results. You know who proposed it. You have examined the investment and the risks.

But have you considered which incentives may have shaped the construction of the proposal? Who will receive recognition if the initiative achieves its objectives? Who will bear the consequences if some assumptions do not materialise?

And above all: if we removed individual incentives and functional results from the evaluation, would this decision remain just as convincing for the organisation as a whole?

If the answer changes, there may be a dimension of risk that the process has not yet made sufficiently visible.

RATIO — Decision Intelligence

Organisations cannot eliminate every distortion of judgement. But they can build processes capable of recognising the interests that influence a proposal and of making visible the differences between individual benefits and collective consequences.

RATIO makes visible the incentive system that helps make a decision desirable, distinguishing the logic of the proposal from the interests that may have shaped how it was built.
